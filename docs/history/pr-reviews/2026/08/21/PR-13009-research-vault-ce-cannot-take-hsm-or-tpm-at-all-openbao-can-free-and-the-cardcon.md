## Description

Two agent results from the hardware push, landed together because each is the other's context.

## Part 1 — "hook HSM/TPM into cert-manager and Vault, if possible"

**Answer: yes for TPM, on the floor that matters — but NOT through Vault.**

**Vault's `seal "pkcs11"` is Enterprise-only** (verbatim: *"Auto-unseal and seal wrapping for PKCS11
require Vault Enterprise"*) **and Vault CE ships no TPM seal type at all.** A TPM can only reach
Vault by pretending to be a PKCS#11 token — landing on the same gated stanza. **Neither hardware path
works on the binary you run.**

**OpenBao — the MPL-2.0 Linux Foundation fork — ships `seal "pkcs11"` with no licence gate**, and
names the contrast itself. So *"we want to replace vault anyways"* turns out to **be** the answer
rather than a workaround. **Both pages re-verified live before landing.** Two load-bearing
conditions are stated: a cgo build is required, and **v2.7.0 moves the seal out of tree** — adopting
it means owning a build or a pin.

**A second, independent gate nobody had noticed:** *Managed Keys* — the feature that would put a **PKI
CA private key** in hardware, as opposed to the barrier key — carries its own ENT badge. **Both gates
are closed on CE.**

**cert-manager:** the complete **24-issuer roster** was pulled. **No PKCS#11 issuer, no HSM issuer, no
TPM issuer.** The in-tree `CA` issuer stores its key *"inside the cluster as a Kubernetes Secret"* —
exactly the property an HSM exists to remove. The one open-source path is `step-issuer` → step-ca.

**And the problem is smaller than it looked:** both ClusterIssuers are ACME Let's Encrypt (still
carrying `email: you@example.com`). **No CA issuer, no private CA — so there is currently no CA
private key in the cluster to protect.**

**A second lying comment, same class as the SPIRE one:** `vault-install.yaml` says *"Vault TLS certs
come from cert-manager"* and sets `tlsDisable: false`, while **nothing wires a cert and the effective
chart default is `tls_disable = 1`.**

### The smallest real step costs nothing and is not a purchase

**Capture one TPM from one cluster node** with the probe that already exists, commit the observed
capture, and delete the **deliberate tripwire test** asserting no such capture exists — whose own
comment says it *"flips to red and is deleted in the same commit that lands the real capture, so the
gap cannot close silently."* That converts *"we can count on the TPM"* from an assumption into a
**measurement**, and answers `secure-boot.nix`'s maintainer-blocked Q4.

Then **one line**: `nodeAttestor.tpmDirect.enabled: true`, keeping `k8sPSAT` during rollout — node
identity moves from *"holder of a ServiceAccount token"* to *"holder of a specific TPM's EK."*

**Physical connectivity resolves rather than argues:** a TPM is already soldered to each node, so the
appointed-hub objection from the connector doc **does not apply to the TPM path at all.**
Architectural, not incidental.

**Test constraint checked, not assumed:** no test asserts on either manifest, so this breaks **zero**
tests on hardware-free machines. And the three-state idiom **already exists** — `Tpm2State` +
`tpm2CheckRan()` — so *"the check did not run"* is already a distinguished value with a predicate.

## Part 2 — HSM procurement after "the nitro can't ship to the US"

**The shipping block is NOT verified, and a harder blocker is confirmed instead.** No public Nitrokey
notice of a US suspension exists; a support thread from four months ago discusses **cost, not
refusal**. Export control is ruled out on evidence — including that **CardContact's product is sitting
in a US warehouse right now.** Likeliest explanation: **a battery banner misread onto the wrong
product** (it's on every Nitrokey page and genuinely blocks their phones/laptops; **the HSM 2 is a
bus-powered CCID token with no battery**).

**What IS confirmed and kills the recommendation on its own: the device is "By inquiry only."** You
cannot buy three off the shelf regardless of shipping. **Zero-cost falsifier:** email sales — they
*are* the inquiry — and one reply resolves stock and shipping together.

### The lead paid off

CardContact names one US distributor, and CardLogix's JSON-LD reads: **SmartCard-HSM 180K USB Token,
$79.26, InStock, add-to-cart.** Same applet Nitrokey resells, **cheaper than the €109 plan it
replaces**, no shipping question, no inquiry gate. secp256k1 confirmed three ways — strongest is the
**OpenSC curve table at `pkcs15-sc-hsm.c:199`**, OID bytes `2B 81 04 00 0A` commented `secp256k1`.
Throughput ≈**12 ops/s** un-hashed, which is the relevant path since x402 submits a pre-hashed
digest: **not the binding constraint.**

| plan | 3 nodes | silicon | availability |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3× YubiHSM 2 | $1,950 | Infineon ×3 | purchasable |
| 3× Nitrokey HSM 2 | ~€327 | NXP JCOP | **by inquiry** |
| **3× SmartCard-HSM via CardLogix** | **$237.78** | **NXP JCOP 3** | **in stock, US** |

**$1,712 cheaper than the monoculture and cheaper than the plan it replaces — there is no
principle-vs-constraint tradeoff here at all.**

### Independent corroboration of the FIPS finding, from a second vendor

Entrust's own algorithm note (02 June 2026), under FIPS 140 Level 3: **"224 minimum; SECP256K1
forbidden; non-named curves forbidden."** Two vendors, two silicon families, two documents. **The
collision is not a Yubico quirk — it is what a FIPS approved-algorithm set does to a non-NIST curve.**

### EUCLEAK worked properly — it kills the mitigation people reach for first

**The vulnerable component was the Infineon crypto library, not Yubico's firmware** — below the
firmware-version boundary, spanning two product lines, surviving ~14 years and ~80 CC evaluations.
**So "different firmware versions across units" would not have helped**; a fleet on 2.3.1/2.3.2/2.3.4
was uniformly vulnerable. **Different silicon vendor is the only mitigation that defeats this class.**

### The treasury/float proposal, evaluated adversarially

**It dies on a fact before the security argument: Coldcard and Jade are Bitcoin-only and cannot hold
USDC.** Only the Trezor and Ledger can be an ERC-20 treasury.

Corrected, the verdict is **demoted, not killed**. The HSM signs 32-byte digests — **no policy engine,
no transaction parser, no notion of recipient or amount.** So the failure mode is not slow drainage:
**the entire float leaves in one transaction within seconds of compromise, and again within seconds of
every refill.** Per-incident loss ≤ float; **total loss = float × refills-before-detection**, so the
bound is set by detection latency — **and the design contains no detector.**

> **The human approval is a rubber stamp BY CONSTRUCTION, not by human weakness.** *"The float is
> empty, top it up?"* is **byte-identical** whether the float went on 4,000 legitimate calls or one
> theft. An approval gate whose input cannot discriminate the attack from normal operation is the
> vacuity class in operational clothing. **Blaming the human would be blaming them for not
> hallucinating evidence.**

Ship it — free, devices owned — but **labelled honestly, and never counted as the off-host velocity
limit.** **It is a smaller bucket, not a valve.** A real bound needs a smart account with an on-chain
spending hook, which x402 does not yet support (open issue #639).

**The one constraint defended without qualification: the treasury device must never be the signing
device, and the approval gate must never be reachable from the signing host.**

No device touched, no session opened, no key/PIN/credential handled, `op` not invoked, nothing applied
to any cluster.
