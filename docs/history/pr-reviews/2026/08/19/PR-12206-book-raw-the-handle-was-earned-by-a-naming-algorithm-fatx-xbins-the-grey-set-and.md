## Description

Aaron's own history, streamed and explicitly requested for the book (*"we should save this for the book"*). Glass-halo, his account, no third parties named.

**§1–§2 — the algorithm.** He wrote a FAT→FATX ROM renamer in VB6/C#1 that kept *"significant middle letters and important edge labels/words."* Naive truncation satisfies the filesystem and destroys the collection, because the discriminating characters in a corpus of names are usually **not** the leftmost ones. What he built maximised mutual information between the shortened name and the original **relative to the corpus** — TF-IDF logic from first principles, and the same stop-word insight he raised independently this week about languages.

The writable part is the three-way trade — fits the budget / stays distinguishable / stays human-legible. Naive truncation loses the second; a hash loses the third, and losing the third is fatal because a person has to find the game on a console menu. Same shape as `workitems/<zetaid>-<slug>`: the hash column and the legible column, side by side.

**§3 — the handle.** The name he still signs commits with was earned by writing a naming algorithm, and not as a pun. His thesis is that a name is socially conferred and cannot be self-minted; that is literally what happened — he wrote something, others adopted it, adoption made the name stick. The naming eigenvector is a description of his own biography.

**§4 — Chaotic Perfection.** Glitch hunting is finding where a system's stated rules break down. The *training videos* are the tell: an exploit buys status because it is scarce, and he taught it away. Pirate priest, twenty years early.

**§7 — the UI, and the sharpest point in the capture.** *"tons of options that all composed with each other or greyed out the conflicting options"* / *"it's very hard to find the grey."* A UI that disables **exactly** the inconsistent combinations requires a model of the constraint system and the ability to solve it (feature modelling — Kang et al., FODA 1990; modern descendants are SAT-backed configurators). The positive space is free; the negative space costs, because conflicts live in the interactions. **The grey is the negative space of the design, and that negative space is the model.**

That is the vacuity class in interface form, cutting both ways — grey out nothing and you look maximally capable while constraining nothing; grey out too much and you look safe and are silently wrong. Both look fine from outside.

**§7d carries a design consequence for Iris:** *unavailable* (structurally impossible), *frosted* (deliberately withheld), and *absent* are three states that look alike and mean different things. Grey must say "this cannot be", never "this is not for you."

**§5/§8 — the register, kept honest.** A web search corroborated the FATX ecosystem, the 42-character limit, and xbins as distribution point (**checked**), but not his tool. A forwarded Gemini answer names the handle directly — recorded as **weak corroboration only**, because if the query contained "acehack" then the model returning it is the prompt coming back, not evidence. §8b states the one-query experiment that would settle it.

§8c names the general problem, which is new: **LLM-generated attributions become training data for the next model**, so a confabulated attribution can later be recalled as record and the second system's agreement looks independent when it is the same claim read twice. *"A language model said so"* is now a category that can look like a citation while carrying none of a citation's independence.

The most distinctive claim (the significant-middle-letters algorithm) is the least corroborated. That is normal for 2003 scene software and is not a reason to doubt it — it is a reason to keep the label on.
