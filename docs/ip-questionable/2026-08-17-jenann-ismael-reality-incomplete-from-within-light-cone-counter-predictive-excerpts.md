## 1. The relativistic claim (the interviewer's framing, then hers)

Jaimungal's framing, opening the episode:

> "The past light cone of any event never contains sufficient information to predict that event
> with certainty."

Ismael's own statement of it, on being given a predictor that knows its entire past light cone and
asked whether that suffices to predict a later system with certainty:

> "No, it doesn't. Why? Because of the way that the light cones are nested inside one another.
> Never sufficient information in the past light cone of the earlier device to predict. Indeed,
> not only to predict what this system is going to do with certainty, in fact to predict anything
> with certainty, because you always need information outside the light cone."

Her stated contrast with the Newtonian case: in Newtonian physics gravitational influence is
instantaneous, so prediction requires the **total** state of the world and there is no mechanism
for determining that. Relativity replaces this with the light-cone structure — which she reads as
*sharpening* the problem for an embedded predictor rather than dissolving it.

The visual form she offers: take the contents of any past light cone, then ask how many models of
the physical theory it can be embedded in, and whether they have different futures. Her answer:
indefinitely many, with very different futures.

## 2. The counter-predictive device

The construction, in her words:

> "A perfectly deterministic device that takes a prediction and does the opposite."

The setup: a predictor is given the laws, the initial conditions, and a perfect physical model of
a target device — but is **required to feed its prediction to the device**, which is programmed to
do the opposite. Deterministic throughout. She notes you can write down such a model.

## 3. The self-referential database

Her "cute little way of putting it": posit an oracle fed the laws of physics and all facts, able to
answer any yes/no question of fact. Stipulate a deterministic universe and effectively computable
laws, so neither computation nor information is the obstacle. Then ask it whether the answer about
to appear in its output channel is "no".

She extends it to a Turing machine programmed with the laws, fed its own Turing number and asked
whether it halts. Her conclusion is **not** that reality is inconsistent:

> "There's no inconsistency in reality, because reality is perfectly consistent. It can say yes,
> it can say no, it can say nothing… No matter what it does, it won't be answering correctly. So
> its view of reality has to be incomplete."

## 4. The conclusion, and the phrase that does the work

> "At any given time, for us, for systems in the world, reality is incomplete. That is, our view
> of the world can't be complete because reality isn't yet complete."

The mechanism she names for it — the phrase she says she developed deliberately for this:

> it can't "stabilize the fact that it's trying to describe independently of the description it
> gives."

And the generalisation to deliberation: predictions guide action, and action is taken *against*
predictions the agent dislikes, so a deliberating agent's representations of the future are
"interfering with themselves." She contrasts this with representations of the past, which she says
are indifferent to how they are represented.

## 5. No totality of facts

Her rejection of the part of the block universe she does not accept: that there is a well-defined
totality of facts, past present and future. She runs the set-theoretic analogue — the set of all
non-self-members — and concludes that a transcendent view of reality would have to be outside
reality, which for any embedded system is unavailable. Her word for the God's-eye totality is a
**mirage** — useful for doing physics, not to be taken fully seriously.

## 6. Loss of a person as loss of information

Recorded because it states, from an unrelated direction, the thesis Zeta is built on. Ismael, on
her parents' deaths, argues that what is lost when a person dies is an irreplaceable structure of
information — culled and curated over a life — and that this loss is **absolute, irretrievable,
and not fungible** with another person's birth. She distinguishes this from Hofstadter's
*I Am a Strange Loop* position that a low-resolution pattern persists in those who knew them,
which she calls right "in a very weak sense": what remains, she says, is a shadow.
