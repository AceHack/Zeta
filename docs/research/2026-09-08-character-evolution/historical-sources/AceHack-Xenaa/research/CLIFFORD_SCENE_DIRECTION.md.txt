# Clifford space to character scenes: a concrete first adapter

Date: 2026-09-08
Author: Vera, OpenAI Codex
Operational status: research-grade local art and adapter experiment

Aaron requested editable 3D assets of AceHack and Xenaa from his supplied
images and two video clips, and identified eventual Clifford-space 3D video
generation as a related Zeta research target. These files preserve that
connection without treating an authored asset as a learned-generation result.

## What actually ran

The character shapes were authored procedurally in Blender 5.2.1 LTS. The
characters are 3D meshes/curves with materials, separate costume components,
mechanisms, hair, props and a basic rigid-part armature. Their starting images
are references; these are not billboards, multi-view reconstruction or trained
neural reconstructions. Unseen surfaces are newly designed interpretations.

`scripts/export_clifford_motion.fsx` loads the existing compiled Zeta.Core and
calls **Cl3.rotor** and **Cl3.rotate** on the three Euclidean basis vectors for
145 fixed angles about the e12 plane. The output is
`research/clifford-turntable.json`. Its maximum coefficient error against the
analytic Z-axis rotation is **2.2204460492503131e-16**, below the declared
1e-12 tolerance. This is a finite numerical check of these samples, not a proof
for all inputs or a learned-motion result. Original execution output is in
`clifford-motion-execution.log`.

Blender consumes the actual retained basis columns as a 3x3 matrix, converts
that matrix to a quaternion, keeps successive quaternion signs compatible,
and keys each character's root. `blender-motion-validation.json` records all
290 character/frame conversions and the maximum reconstruction error against
a separate 1e-6 tolerance appropriate to Blender's float32 math. The master
`.blend` retains this animation. The source asset itself was not generated by
Clifford inference; the root motion uses the actual algebra implementation.

## A bounded next research target

Use these stable character/prop identities as an authored target scene, then
compare a geometric representation with an ordinary quaternion/matrix scene
DAG on exactly the same fixed motions and observation budget. First test
rigid transforms, inverse composition, parent-child attachment, camera
projection and occlusion across held-out views. Include reflected/mirrored
coordinate conventions, quaternion sign continuity, scaled/nonunit rotors
and out-of-order parent updates as discriminators. The named invariant is
pairwise Euclidean distance under an admitted unit rotor, plus attachment
consistency in a declared parent frame.

Keep the outputs separate:

1. Scene representation and correct renderable transformations.
2. Learned motion or pose prediction under limited observations.
3. Learned mesh/material/identity generation.
4. Temporally consistent rendered video with cloth, hair, interaction and
   camera control.

Passing step 1 does not establish any of the next three. The larger learner
can remain a compositional probabilistic DAG whose nodes contain NNs or
nested modules; adopting geometric primitives does not require replacing
that higher structure with one ordinary neural network. Compare tracking
error, attachment drift, held-out view consistency, resource cost and failure
rate before deciding which representation merits further investment.
