## Verbatim (Yann LeCun; timestamps dropped, spoken text faithful)

I'll talk about world models — possibly the enabler for the next AI revolution. There's a lot of machine-learning people in the room. I have bad news for you: machine learning sucks. If we compare the learning abilities of machines with humans and animals, clearly there's a big gap. People and animals can learn new tasks extremely quickly, with very few trials, very few samples. People have common sense, animals too — physical common sense. There's a lot of tasks we can accomplish zero-shot even if we've never faced them before. How do we do this with machines? We have very powerful AI techniques that everybody is using, but they don't really handle the real world. They don't handle continuous, high-dimensional, noisy data. Language is easy by comparison. The real world is messy; language is simple.

This connects with what Vladlen said earlier, and Gitendra as well — the Moravec paradox: things that are simple are difficult for computers, and things that are complicated for humans turn out to not be that difficult for computers — like playing chess, computing integrals symbolically, solving equations, proving math theorems. How is it that a 10-year-old can basically do what you'd like a domestic robot to do, without being trained — the first time you ask, they can do it (they may not want to, but they can)? How come any teenager can learn to drive a car in a few hours of practice, yet the self-driving car companies have literally millions of hours of training data, and despite that they can't get a machine to imitate humans and drive at the same level of reliability? Otherwise we'd have level-five self-driving cars, and we don't. At best, in consumer cars, we have level two or three; the robotaxis are very heavily engineered with various sensors. We keep bumping into the Moravec paradox, and we really have to go beyond this.

If you believe intelligence requires grounding — some philosophers and some language people don't believe that's necessary, but I think it is. Like Vladlen, we're in Switzerland; outside, Jean Piaget was a big influence on me. He had a debate with Noam Chomsky in France in the late 1970s about whether language was innate or learned. One participant was Seymour Papert — a professor at MIT, talking about the perceptron, saying those simple machine-learning models are capable of learning surprisingly complex tasks, which may be evidence that learning is possible, contrary to what Chomsky was saying. Ten years before that, Papert had co-written a book that basically killed the entire field of neural nets by pointing out the limitations of the perceptron — yet here he was, ten years later, arguing those things were interesting to study.

Piaget says: intelligence is not what you know, it's what you do when you don't know. (He never actually said this — it's apocryphal, distilled by other psychologists, but he's quoted as saying it.) Intelligence is not an accumulation of declarative knowledge. LLMs are an accumulation of declarative knowledge — the main reason they're useful is they can accumulate a lot of it. Intelligence is not a collection of skills, either. You can build a machine to accomplish almost any task if you spend enough resources, including self-driving. But that's not what intelligence is. Intelligence is the ability to learn a new task with very little training, to accomplish new tasks. That's what Piaget means. And what that means is we're not going to have any simple measures of intelligence — because any particular task, with enough effort and time, you can crack. It's more about how adaptive you are. The notion of AGI is complete nonsense. Human intelligence is specialized. The characterization of human intelligence is that it's very quickly adaptive — we can learn new tasks. All of us know different sets of knowledge and have different skills, because we've been exposed to different environments and had to solve different problems. We're adaptive. That's really what intelligence is.

How do humans and animals learn? A lot of learning takes place in the early months of life, mostly by observation. A two-month-old can develop a dynamical model of its own limbs but basically cannot affect the world. Yet it can learn a lot. One thing a baby learns really quickly is that the world is three-dimensional — because the fact that every point in the world has a distance from us is the best way to explain how our view of the world changes when we move our head. Babies are being moved, they see parallax, and derive that the world is three-dimensional. We can do this with learning machines today — they learn the world is 3D only by being exposed passively to videos.

Basic concepts like object permanence run really quickly; notions of stability, rigidity. But intuitive physics — inertia, gravity — takes about nine months for human infants, shorter for most animals. Put an 8-or-9-month-old in a high chair with toys and the child will systematically throw all the toys on the floor and watch the result — doing the experiment that gravity applies to everything. But they can also learn gravity by observation: show a scenario where a car is pushed off a platform and appears to float; a six-month-old barely pays attention (hasn't learned gravity yet), a ten-month-old is very surprised. That's how psychologists measure whether a baby has learned a concept — violation of expectation — and we can use those techniques to test whether a machine-learning system has acquired notions of common sense. Gitendra and I collaborated on a paper here, mostly written by Emmanuel Dupoux.

What is intelligence, if not an accumulation of skills or declarative knowledge? The ability to accomplish new tasks, solve new problems, without prior training. AGI makes no sense as a phrase; human intelligence is specialized. The question is not "do you know how to do everything" but "can you learn quickly how to do anything," a wide spectrum of things.

A simple calculation. There are still a lot of people, particularly on the US west coast, who believe we'll reach AGI by scaling up LLMs — maybe training on synthetic data, a few post-training and reinforcement-learning tricks. I think that's impossible; I'm a believer in grounded intelligence. A typical LLM today is trained on about 20 trillion words, ~30 trillion tokens, each token ~3 bytes — data volume about 10^14 bytes. That would take a human about 400,000 years to read. Compare with a four-year-old: about 16 hours of wake time, a small amount of video (about 30 minutes of YouTube uploads). We have two million optic-nerve fibers carrying about one byte per second each. The data volume a four-year-old has seen through vision (and touch) is about 10^14 bytes. A four-year-old, through vision, has seen the same amount of data as 400,000 years through all the human-produced text on the internet. We're not going to get anything like human-like intelligence by training on text alone.

You'll say video is much more redundant than text. But that's a feature, not a bug. To train a system, particularly with self-supervised learning, you need redundancy. If you don't have redundancy you can't learn anything. So redundancy is good — you don't want too much of it, though.

Another question: the right properties of intelligent systems. An important property is the mode of inference. Does it compute its output by propagating through a fixed number of layers of a neural net — or consider the alternative: computing the output by searching for an output most compatible with the input. You observe a situation, run it through a perception module that produces a representation of the current state of the world; you can directly produce an action (a reactive system), or you can imagine an action and have an intelligent system figure out: is this a good action for this observation, will it accomplish the task I want? The objective characterizes whether the task has been accomplished — think of it as a cost function. It's not used for learning, it's used for inference. Think of it as negative likelihood in a probabilistic model, or — as I prefer — an energy function. Inference is the process of searching for an output that minimizes some energy function at inference time. That's intrinsically more powerful computationally than propagation through a fixed number of layers.

Contrast: the LLM-like model takes a window of inputs, runs through a fixed number of layers of a big neural net (a few hundred billion parameters), produces one token, shifts it into the input, produces the next, etc. — autoregressive prediction, a fixed amount of computation per token. This is not a good model of reasoning. The way you coerce an LLM to reason is you trick it into generating more tokens. But that's not how we reason — we reason internally, not in token space, not in language. Compare the model on the right: you perceive the environment, get the current state, imagine a sequence of actions (a proposal), feed it to an internal world model which predicts the outcome, feed that to an objective that measures whether the task is accomplished; by optimization you search for an action sequence that minimizes this energy at inference time. In my opinion that's a much more powerful model. But you need a world model.

I settled on this architecture about five years ago; I wrote a long paper, put it online in 2022. It's based on the idea that reasoning and planning are essential, and proceed by energy minimization rather than forward propagation — and for that you need a world model. You observe the environment, the perception module produces a representation of the initial state (only what you currently perceive), so you may have to combine it with the content of a memory to get a complete idea of the state of the world. Feed this to your world model with a proposal for an action sequence; the world model predicts the outcome; feed it to an objective (energy function) — zero if the task is accomplished, positive otherwise, perhaps measuring distance to accomplishment. You can have another set of objectives that are GUARDRAILS — ensuring whatever state sequences the system takes the world through won't kill or hurt anyone or have deleterious effects. A system constructed this way can be made intrinsically safe, because it has to obey and optimize the guardrail objective with every output it produces. This is not the case for an LLM — the only way to make an LLM safe is fine-tuning, and there's always a way to jailbreak it. Here you can't jailbreak the system; it can do nothing but optimize the guardrail objectives and the task objective.

With a world model you can apply it multiple time steps; each action sequence decomposes into a sequence, and the guardrails apply to all the steps. Planning by optimization this way is akin to model predictive control (MPC) — classical optimal control going back to the 1960s. Ultimately you want hierarchical planning. All of us do it; animals do it. Say I'm in my office at NYU and want to be in Paris tomorrow. There's no way I can plan the entire trip in terms of 10-millisecond muscle actions — it's too long and I don't have the information (I don't know how long I'll wait for a taxi). So I plan hierarchically: high level — get to the airport, catch a plane (a two-step plan, no details needed). Now a sub-goal: be at the airport, which means go down to the street and hail a taxi. Now: get down to the street, which means walk to the elevator, push the button, get down, walk out the door. And so on down the hierarchy, until the action is simple and familiar — you can stand up from your chair without thinking; that could be just a policy. Ultimately we want systems to do hierarchical planning. This is an unsolved problem. If you're starting a PhD in AI for robotics or agentic AI, this is a great, completely open topic — nobody has proved they know how to do it.

How are we going to train those models? Let's say non-hierarchical to start. The natural instinct these days is to train a generative model. I've been trying to train world-model-like things for about 15 years, mostly failing for the first 10 — because I was trying to train generative models. What's a generative model? Self-supervised learning has been astonishingly successful for language: take a string of words, remove some, corrupt the input, run it through a big neural net, train it to recover the missing parts. That works amazingly well for text — original models like BERT did this, and an LLM is a special case where the only word you remove is the last. It scales if you do it right. But it doesn't work for video. Show the initial segment of a video and ask it to predict what happens next at the pixel level — it doesn't really work; the representations are not particularly good. The reason: you cannot predict everything in a video. In text it's easy — a finite number of words, so you produce a probability distribution over the dictionary. You can't do that with video — an incredibly large number of possible frames. If I take a video of this room, slowly rotate the camera, stop, and ask the system to continue — it might predict we're in a classroom, finite size, maybe windows on one side — but there's no way it can predict what all of you look like, or which chairs are unoccupied. You just don't have the information. So when you train a system to make this prediction, you kill it.

You'll say we can train systems to produce cute videos — video generation. Yes, but that prediction is usually done in representation space, not pixel space; a second stage turns predictions into high-resolution video, and the system only needs to produce one cute-looking video — it doesn't need to represent all plausible videos, a much simpler problem. Ten years ago we tried to train neural nets to predict short video clips; you get blurry predictions, because the system predicts the average of everything that can happen. You can correct that with latent-variable models like diffusion — which produce cute videos. Do they understand the world? The evidence is no.

My solution is an architecture called Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture — JEPA. On the left, a generative architecture: you observe X (maybe an action A) and the result Y, and the system reconstructs Y in its most minute detail. With JEPA you observe X and Y and A, but you ENCODE both X and Y, and the prediction takes place in that representation space. The system can eliminate from the input — by constructing a representation of Y — all the information about Y that is simply not predictable. That makes the prediction more abstract, with fewer details, but more accurate.

How do you train a generative model? Easy — the cost is a reconstruction cost; you can train it as an autoencoder, but then you need to restrict the information content in the code, or as a denoising autoencoder (corrupt the input, train to recover the original) — diffusion models are a special case of denoising. The bad news: when you train systems of this type to learn representations of images, you don't get good representations; feed them to a downstream supervised task, the results are not great. To get good results you have to use joint-embedding architectures. All the best self-supervised image/video systems use joint embedding; none use reconstruction.

Either you have two views of the same scene and train a neural net to produce representations you want to be identical, or you use the corruption technique — corrupt an input and train JEPA to predict the representation of the original from the representation of the corrupted version. There's a big issue: the system can COLLAPSE. (Generative models can collapse too — an autoencoder without a restriction on the code learns the identity function.) A JEPA can collapse by completely ignoring the inputs and producing constant representations, making the prediction problem trivial. So if you just train to minimize prediction error, it collapses. The whole trick of self-supervised joint-embedding learning is how you PREVENT collapse.

My favorite concept to prevent collapse is information maximization. You come up with an objective that measures the information content of the representation coming out of your encoders and maximize it (your cost is minus the information). There's a bunch of techniques over the last six or seven years — MCR, MCR², VICReg, Barlow Twins. Barlow Twins and VICReg come from people working with me; MCR from Berkeley, MCR² from a neuroscience colleague at NYU. JEPA is gaining popularity — about 1,700 papers mention "joint embedding predictive architecture" on Google Scholar.

There's an issue: how do you measure information content? You need a differentiable measure so you can backpropagate and maximize it. Bad news: we don't have objective measures of information content — the proper definitions require knowing the distribution of the vectors, and we only have samples coming out of an encoder. How do you compute information content from a finite number of samples? Second problem: to maximize something you'd need a LOWER bound on information content, so that maximizing pushes the actual content up. But every empirical measure we have is an UPPER bound. So what do we do? We come up with a good upper bound and cross our fingers — and we show some theorems.

The proper way to explain how you train self-supervised systems — and really every learning system — is a framework I call energy-based models, which I've advocated for 20 years. To capture the dependency between two variables X and Y where there's no functional relationship (no single Y for a given X — a dependency, a relation, not a function): learn a contrast/energy function that tells you whether a point in XY space is near the training data or not. Think of a landscape where the data points are in a valley (in Switzerland, a lake), and as you move out the altitude (energy) goes up. If I give you a value for X, you can infer values of Y that minimize the energy — inference by optimization, not forward propagation — and possibly the other way around. In situations like video prediction with an infinite number of possible answers, the proper way to train is energy-based models. Probabilistic models are a special case where the energy has a particular form and a particular loss function — so it's a slightly more general framework than probabilistic inference and learning.

To train an energy-based model you have to prevent collapse — manifested by the energy function being flat everywhere (zero everywhere, like an autoencoder learning the identity, or a JEPA producing constant representations). To prevent collapse, do one of two things. One: contrastive methods — generate points outside the data region and push their energy up. Two (which I prefer): regularized methods that work by minimizing the volume of space that can take low energy — if you push down the energy of certain regions, the rest has to go up, because there's only a small volume of low energy to go around.

Back to information maximization. Run a batch of samples through an encoder; you get a matrix where each row is the representation for one sample, each column is the value of one variable across all samples. Two ways to make that matrix informative: make all the rows different, or make all the columns different. You want the columns different — if all columns are the same, every variable carries the same information, not very informative; you want each variable maximally disentangled from the others, giving independent information. That's a "dimension-contrastive" (regularized) method. Making the rows all different is a sample-contrastive method — very popular for some applications; a lot of LLM perceptual pipelines are trained with CLIP, a contrastive method doing joint embedding between images and text. But I prefer the other one.

The idea that you need an abstract representation to make predictions is very natural — we do it as humans, scientists, engineers; animals do it. In principle I could simulate everything in this room at the level of quantum field theory or particle physics — every particle's trajectory, down to all our brain processes — and figure out whether any of you understands me or is asleep. But that's completely impractical. In science we invent abstractions that let us make predictions while ignoring details of the underlying state — from quantum fields to particles, atoms, molecules, proteins, cells, organisms, individuals, societies, ecosystems. Every level is an abstraction that allows longer-range predictions than the level below by ignoring details. Which is why understanding what goes on in this room is better done at the level of psychology than particle physics. Physicists make fun of everyone ("you just applied physics") — but there's specific knowledge about chemistry that doesn't derive directly from physics; the abstraction contains new structure not apparent below. JEPA builds on this — you need an abstraction to make predictions. To design an airplane you do computational fluid dynamics — model the air in every little cube by velocity and density and solve Navier–Stokes — ignoring a huge amount of detail (molecules bumping into each other). You never simulate fluids at the molecular level; it's too complicated and would diverge from reality quickly because it has too many details. You have to ignore details to make accurate long-term predictions.

So world models should NOT be simulators; they should work in abstract space. They should NOT be digital twins — that's a buzz phrase. They should definitely NOT be generative models. And they should NOT be video generation. A lot of people working on video generation call it world models — it's not; they're video-generation systems. One big message: if you want world models, do NOT work on video generation. If you want cute videos, work on video generation. But if you want to control robots or industrial processes or understand the world, do not work on generation.

You want models to control complex systems where you cannot model the dynamics by writing equations. A humanoid robot you can write dynamical equations for and simulate somersaults and kung fu — that's simple. As soon as a robot interacts with the real world, it's much more complicated and harder to reduce to equations. Think of a turbojet, a chemical plant, a patient, or a robot interacting with the real world in complex ways — you can't reduce it to a small number of equations. You have to learn a phenomenological model of the whole system and its interaction with the environment, so you can make predictions and plan a sequence of actions to reach an outcome. That's a world model. The concept is old — it goes back to the 1960s, the root of optimal control.

Now a particular technique I'm fond of, which I think we'll expand over the next months and years, for information maximization — called SIGReg (Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization). Run a batch of samples through your encoders; you get points in a vector space of dimension whatever your representation is. Make the distribution of those points an isotropic Gaussian with the same variance in all dimensions — because an isotropic Gaussian is a distribution where all variables are independent (maximally informative individually), and it has maximum entropy for a given variance (we don't care about that as much) — what's interesting is it makes the variables independent. How? We don't have the distribution, just points, in a high-dimensional space (say 2,000 dimensions) with a few hundred or thousand points. The trick: project the points along a single direction, getting a marginal distribution; compute the empirical cumulative distribution (a staircase, since you have discrete points); measure the distance between that staircase and the cumulative distribution of a Gaussian. For every point you can tell if it's to the left or right of the ideal Gaussian — that gives a gradient (move the point this way or that). Do this along lots and lots of directions; there's a theorem that in the limit your joint distribution becomes isotropic Gaussian. So do many projections, compute the gradients, move the points / backpropagate so the overall distribution gets more Gaussian.

We've applied this to training world models that are action-conditioned and used them for planning — it works decently. The source code is available; it's very simple, you can train it on one GPU. We need to scale it up. There's a theoretical paper we put out a few days ago: if you assume the underlying data distribution is actually an isotropic Gaussian, and the observations are a complicated nonlinear transformation of those points (like a spiral), then a neural net trained with SIGReg will recover the original Gaussian in representation space — not a general proof, but a proof that if your explanatory variables are Gaussian, the system recovers them up to a rotation.

There's another set of techniques worth mentioning because they work really well and have been scaled up — based on distillation. You still have two encoders (a JEPA architecture); take an input, transform/corrupt/mask it, train the system to predict in representation space — but you don't propagate gradient through the encoder on the right. The two encoders have identical architectures and share weights, but the right one uses an exponential moving average of the left one's weights (the left gets gradient and updates all the time; the right updates slower). This is derived from ideas at Google DeepMind for stabilizing variance in reinforcement learning — applied to self-supervised learning from images, they called it BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent). A whole bunch of methods from Meta (SimSiam, etc.) use this EMA idea. A method called I-JEPA produced really good results; we compared it with a generative approach (MAE — masked autoencoder) — not only better but much faster to train. Another technique is DINO — done by former colleagues at Meta in Paris; completely self-supervised, joint embedding, distillation with various tricks and a lot of engineering. These produce the best generic representations of images today — for any vision task, probably the best encoder. We've used DINO as an encoder and then trained a world model and done planning. [Shows a video: an initial state of a simulated environment with complex dynamics, goals at top; at bottom, a planner using the trained world model gets the world to a configuration as close as possible to the goal in fewer than 25 steps — applied to double pendulum, push-T, etc.]

More recently we applied it to video: take a video, mask a big chunk, train the JEPA to produce good representations so it can predict the representation of the full video from the partially masked one. Once trained, use the encoder to extract features and train a head on top for some task — it works at state-of-the-art for traditional vision tasks from video, like action recognition and prediction. One interesting thing: VJEPA has learned some level of common sense. Because we trained it to predict what happens next in a video, we can measure its internal prediction error — show it a video and monitor the error at every time step (a window of 16 frames slid along the video). If you show a video where something impossible or unphysical occurs, the prediction error shoots through the roof — like the little girl looking at the car that doesn't fall. A ball is thrown and disappears: prediction error shoots up. That's interesting because it's the first time, from my point of view, I've seen a completely self-supervised system acquire some level of common sense — telling you what's possible and what's not.

Remember babies learn the world is 3D because it's the best way to explain how the view changes when they move their head. We took the representation learned by a version called VJEPA-2 and trained a head on top to predict depth from a single image — it does a really good job, better than DINOv3. That shows the system, just by being trained to fill in the blanks in videos at the representation level, basically understands the world is three-dimensional ("understands" with double quotes), and the notion of object — use the representation as input to a segmentation system and it works decently.

Let me conclude. Abandon generative models. (If you work on LLMs — you should not work on LLMs, at least in academia; there's nothing you can bring to the table.) Abandon generative models in favor of joint-embedding architectures if you're interested in intelligence. Abandon probabilistic models in favor of energy-based models. I made an argument for regularized methods / information maximization through variables instead of samples — so contrastive methods, which have a lot of practical applications. I've been saying forever to abandon reinforcement learning — I don't really mean abandon, I mean minimize its use, because it's horribly inefficient in sample efficiency; RL is what you do when you're desperate and there's nothing else you can do. Do most of the learning by observation — learning world models — and once you have good representations, you can use RL on top without too many samples. If you're interested in real progress in AI for the real world — physical AI — don't work on LLMs, and don't work on generative models either. As you can guess, this does not make me very popular in Silicon Valley. I left Meta at the end of last year and formed a new company, Emmy Labs (AMI Labs), for AI for the real world — physical AI; robotics is a use case but it's not just that — control of industrial processes, anything high-dimensional, continuous, and noisy, for which LLMs are completely helpless. That's it. Thank you very much.

If your task is to open a door — I'm talking about a task objective now — you need a cost function to tell you whether the door is open, and that might have to be trained when you train to accomplish the task.
