So here are some questions that I would like to address. What are world models? That's one of the main topics of this workshop. And what is a first-person perspective, and how those two go together. And then finally, can machines have it?

So let's go to what are world models. And I'm gonna go back to Kenneth Craik, who was a Scottish philosopher and a psychologist, and he wrote this book in 1943, *The Nature of Explanation*. And I think he has really the best sort of overall picture of world models. So that's gonna be my basic definition. Now, notice this book was written in 1943, which is the same year as the McCulloch-Pitts paper comes out on neurons. So it's very interesting about this. He also sadly died very young. On bicycle — a car crashed into him. And so he may not be that well known, but should be.

So this is Craik's definition of world model, and he even uses the words early on. He says — and I have some numbers here which I put in because I think they're interesting points he's making — "If the organism carries a small scale model of external reality and its own possible actions within its head, it is able to, one, try out various alternatives; two, conclude which is the best of them; three, react to future situations before they arise; four, utilize the knowledge of past events in dealing with the present and the future; and five, in every way to react in a much fuller, safer, and more competent manner to the emergencies which face it." So I think this is very contemporary for today. And in fact, for me, this is a very good working definition for a world model. I would like to thank Aran Nayebi, who actually introduced me to Craik. And really, I'm hoping that many of you will look into this too, because I think it's extremely relevant.

So what is a first-person perspective? Now I'm going to go to Francisco Varela, who is a neuroscientist. He got his PhD with Torsten Wiesel at Harvard; Maturana was his mentor. He's a Chilean neuroscientist. He had a laboratory in Paris, and I understand that William was actually in that laboratory. And what he says is, "By first-person events, we mean the lived experience associated with cognitive and mental events. Sometimes terms such as phenomenal consciousness and even qualia are also used, but it is natural to speak of conscious experience or simply experience. These terms imply here that the process being studied — vision, pain, memory, imagination, etc. — have a subjective side." So, of course, what he's talking about is the hard problem of consciousness, and I'm gonna equate first-person perspective in this talk with the hard problem of consciousness.

So many view the hard problem as needing a new kind of science to explain subjective experience, and we view the hard problem as a challenge to show that subjective consciousness surely is computational.

Can machines have it? Now I'm going to go back to Craik again and read what he has to say. He says, "Assuming then the existence of the external world" — and I put in *world model* — "I have outlined a symbolic theory of thought in which the nervous system is viewed as a calculating machine" — the same year as '43, as the McCulloch-Pitts paper — "capable of modeling or paralleling external events, and have suggested that the process of paralleling is a basic feature of thought and of explanation. The possessor of a nervous system is thus able to anticipate events instead of making invariable empirical trial." So this is his notion about the nervous system being sort of like — he calls it a calculating machine. This is '43; that's his terminology. And I'm gonna replace in what he says here, the *conscious Turing machine*, which I'm gonna talk about — our machine model for consciousness or first-person perspective. So: assuming the existence of the external world and world model, the conscious Turing machine is viewed as capable of modeling or paralleling external events. The possessor of a CTM brain is thus able to anticipate events instead of making invariable empirical trial. So I'm adapting Craik again here, in kind of a pre-justification of what we're doing.

So can machines have it? Here are some specs for our conscious Turing machine. I'm not going to go through the formal list, but we've defined it as a seven-tuple. So we're inspired in part by Alan Turing's simple yet powerful formal model of computation, the Turing machine, and Bernard Baars' global workspace theory of consciousness, often called the theater model. And so the CTM, or the Conscious Turing Machine, is our candidate for a simple, formal, theoretical computer science model of consciousness, which at a high level explains a number of features of human conscious phenomena and aligns at a high level with many of the major theories of consciousness.

But before I go ahead, I want to make sure I'm clear on this. The CTM is not a standard Turing machine. What gives the CTM its feeling of consciousness is not its input-output map, nor its computing power, but what it's doing under the hood, how it's operating. And the CTM is not a standard global workspace model — the global workspace model is more of a metaphor. This is a formal TCS model. It has a well-defined competition for processors to get their information into short-term memory on stage. It has no central executive. And its subjective feelings come, in addition to the global workspace, from the co-evolution of Brainish — which is CTM's self-generated, multimodal or multisensory internal language — and its model of the world. So its feelings are coming out of this. And then finally, the CTM is not a model of the brain. It's way too simple for that.

So, with all that qualification, let me give you a little bit of the architecture before I go into the construction of Brainish. So what is STM for our model? It's the stage. It's a broadcasting system, and it can hold one chunk of information at a time. George Miller talked about the magic number seven plus or minus two; we're looking at, for simplicity, one chunk, and so one will do for us. It's like a broadcast. And LTM will be the audience — a huge audience. We're assuming like 10 to the 7th processors, or maybe 2 to the 24th — we're looking binary for our calculations. All the CTM processors are going to be included in LTM. For simplicity, all the processors are in LTM; STM is just a broadcasting station, not a processor.

At T equals zero, almost all the processors are generic. They're powerful and generic, but we do have some that are built in. So we have input processors (sensory processors), output processors (motor processors), gauge processors (homeostats — like a fuel gauge). And all others are generic, but we build in a few. To start, for simplicity, all the processors are unlinked, and links will form as time goes on. We have predictive learning algorithms built into the processors. An important one we have are Avrim Blum's sleeping-expert algorithms, which help balance the weights that processors give their information that they want to get up into short-term memory onto the stage. And each processor has its own internal language, but the processors communicate in Brainish — CTM's self-generated multimodal language. What this language does is it fuses words (like English words), images, sounds, smells, sensations. For example, a Brainish label for a rose might be a fusion of the red color, the sweet smell, and the soft touch of the petal — all fused together in a Brainish word.

Now let me go to the up-tree, down-tree, and links. The up-tree is a conduit for the competition to get into STM. At every moment of time, every processor puts in a chunk into a competition. This competition is very much like a tennis match, and as the chunk wins at one level, it moves up the next level, and so forth. In the literature, chunk is often informally defined as a few words, maybe a sentence, a small concept. But we have to formally define it, so we define it as a four-plus tuple. It starts with the address of the processor that put in the information (if there are 10^7 processors, that's like a seven-digit number); the time (could be a ten-digit number); the gist (succinct information in Brainish — one can think of the gist as like a frame in a dream: a picture, some color, some movement, a fear, a nightmare — all those sensations together would be in a Brainish gist); and the weight, the valence number — plus or minus — the importance or value of the confidence that the processor gives its gist.

It's like a tennis match, but it's fairer. The competition takes the place of having a central executive deciding who's the winner — and we think a central executive is not a very good thing: it could be biased, it's not omnipotent. So we feel the competition is a better replacement, because it can take in lots of information, it can have outlier information get into the stage and be broadcast, it can discover latent talents. So we actually think it's a good model for AGI also. As a chunk moves up the competition, the first four components stay the same, but the auxiliary information slowly gathers more and more global information. So when one gets the winner on top, we have the original information plus a lot of global information.

We were determined — particularly Manuel was determined — that this would be a totally deterministic model. But we found that to keep it deterministic (and we're also looking for simplicity, parsimony), every time we wanted to do something, there'd be just so many details. The moment we put what we call a coin-toss neuron at each of the nodes as the chunk is going up, everything became very simple. So a little bit of probability here simplified the whole model. And in fact, the winner — this is fairer than a tennis tournament — the winner is going to win with probability proportional to its rank compared to the sum of the other ranks. In a tennis match, whoever you play with first is gonna determine a lot. In our model, the winner is determined by its weight over the sum of the other weights, the rank over the sum of the other ranks — independent of location. Its winning probability is independent of location, which is not true in a tennis or chess match because who you play with first determines a lot of your future.

Now, the down-tree is the conduit for global broadcasting. As soon as a chunk wins, in the next tick it's broadcast all-to-all simultaneously to all long-term memory processors. All the processors are getting a lot of information: the address of the processor that put in the winning chunk, the gist, the valence, and the auxiliary information collecting more and more global information, so they have a sense of where everybody was to start with.

Built into the long-term memory processors: when they get these broadcasted chunks, they store them — all of them. We call that the global Brainish dictionary. All the processors have the same global Brainish dictionary — they're storing all the chunks coming down through the global broadcast (not the ones they get by link). And we can define a Brainish word to be a pointer to the chunk. So if later on they're given a word PT, they can look it up in their dictionary. That's the word; PT is the pointer to the meaning. And a Brainish gist will be a sequence of at most eight words — we want it succinct.

We also have triggers built in. If a broadcasted chunk's absolute value of its weight is higher than some threshold, then all processors have to unpack the gist — go find the meaning of the gist, decipher what it's really telling them. And if that weight is negative — not just large in absolute value, but negative — all processors must try to counter extreme negativity. And then, there are no links to start with, but we do have a Hebbian-like principle that processors that broadcast together link together. What links do is turn slow communication (that has to go through short-term memory) into fast communication through long-term memory processors — what we call the unconscious processes. When you learn to ride a bike, all your motions are very conscious; you have to think about them. But once you learn, your motor processor is connected with your vision processor and most everything is done automatically, unconsciously — unless a car comes at you.

Now the input-output maps. The CTM is not a brain in a vat. This is very important for us. It has input maps that take information from the external world through sensors to input (sensory) processors. And it has output processors that give information to actuators that can affect the outside world. There are cycles of prediction, feedback, and learning in the CTM — what we call predictive dynamics. Processors make predictions (for example, when they put chunks into the competition), they get feedback (for example, from broadcasts), and then there's learning happening within the processors themselves.

So now I'm ready for the very first definition. I'll have two definitions of consciousness. The first is conscious attention. In the consciousness community this is often thought of as access consciousness, but we call it conscious attention. We say conscious attention in the CTM is the simultaneous reception by all long-term memory processors of the global broadcast of the winner. When they all get the information of the winner, we're saying the CTM is paying conscious attention to that broadcast. And so we have an axiom — our first axiom: global broadcast evokes a unitary experience. When all the processors get the same information in the CTM, it is a unitary experience. It's related to the binding problem in consciousness science. We call this the global broadcast axiom, or the conscious attention axiom. And you can see there's something like the Libet effect, because the winning chunk was put in at time t; it took H steps (if it's a binary tree of height 24, then 24+1 ticks happen). So the winning chunk was put in 24+1 ticks before the CTM paid conscious attention to it — a little bit of what we know in the brain, that some decisions are made before we're paying conscious attention. So there's a delay between submission of winner and conscious attention. The reasonableness of our definitions or axioms lies in the number of phenomena associated with consciousness that the model can exhibit and explain easily and naturally, and agrees at a high level with cognitive neuroscience explanations — Libet effect; in our PNAS paper, blindsight, inattentional blindness, change blindness.

So now we have attention, but what about the subjectiveness — this first-person subjective experience? We're saying attention is not all you need. What we need is the co-evolution of Brainish and the model of the world in the CTM. The model-of-the-world processor maintains models of the world and CTM in these worlds. It and its models are widely distributed; for simplicity, we're assuming every processor has the model-of-the-world processor in it. It's a useful fiction to think of it as a single processor, but that's not the case. What the model-of-the-world processor does is label sketches in the model of the world with multimodal Brainish gist, including labeling sketches of actuators as *self* when the CTM consistently detects the actuators carrying out its requests. Here is an example of a little baby who, by power of thought, is moving its left leg, and in its model of the world it's labeling its left leg as self. It tries the right leg, can't quite do it yet, but its left leg gets labeled self.

Let me go specifically to how the model of the world is being created. The newborn CTM starts out with a foggy model of the world. CTM's first broadcasted chunk we'll call the initial sketch — some processor P0 at time zero (it didn't have any language, so just the nil word) with some weight got into short-term memory and got broadcast. That very first chunk we call the initial sketch. Sketches in general will be broadcasted chunks. We give a name to this sketch — the first two components of the chunk, P0-0. In English, think of it as blob zero. P0-0 is a pointer: if any processor ever sees that word, it can look it up and know it was connected to the very first broadcasted chunk. That's its meaning — the first thing it ever knew. And Brainish words are such pointers (there's something similar in large language models — key going to value; don't stress that too much).

Now shortly, a primal word for pain will be created. If you've ever witnessed a birth — there's a program on PBS on midwives — the baby is born and cries and everybody's happy. Why is everybody happy when the baby's crying? There are gauges built in — something like an oxygen gauge — and that oxygen gauge is low. To survive, the infant (or CTM) needs oxygen. So it puts in a chunk into the competition with increasingly high weights until it goes above threshold. It's a negative valence, very high negative, and all processors know they need to do something to get that negative weight down — but they don't know what to do. So they just do what they do. The motor processor attached to arms and legs makes them flail; the baby pees, poos; the vocal actuator screams and cries. The cry opens the lungs, the infant takes its first breath, and the oxygen gauge can go down. That's why everybody's happy — it's breathing.

So let's see what that word is. Suppose P1 is the address of the oxygen gauge, and at time T1 it put in a chunk (it didn't have much to say — just nil — but for the very large negative weight that got everybody doing something, above threshold). It got down, all the processors see it. We'll say P1-T1 will be the Brainish word for what we call in English *pain one* — it's pointing to that chunk. P1 is the oxygen gauge at time T1. So a Brainish gist is a string of at most eight Brainish words. We already have two: P0-0 ("blob zero") and P1-T1 ("pain"). So we have a gist: "blob zero is in pain." You can imagine the CTM using that gist again and again in situations. Brainish sketches, words, and labels continue to evolve and build on earlier ones — when this infant becomes a toddler and names himself "big boy self" and he has skinned-knee pain, that's an evolved feeling of pain built on earlier pains.

Here's another definition: we say CTM unpacks a chunk when all processors simultaneously take note of the weight and simultaneously retrieve the meanings of all words appearing in the gist. So it's gotta do more than look at the chunk — it's gotta look at the gist, its words, and the meanings simultaneously (which it can look up because it has them). That's what it unpacks — the fused meaning of all the words in the gist. And we say conscious awareness arises in the CTM when the CTM unpacks a broadcasted chunk. Our first axiom: global broadcast gives a unitary experience. Now our second axiom: global unpacking gives a unique subjective experience. When the CTM does global unpacking, a unique fusion of the subjective experience evoked by the meanings of the words in the gist is evoked. It's an axiom that's kind of inductive: unpacking a chunk evokes in CTM a unique subjective experience; we start with the primal ones (unpacking a primal chunk evokes a unique primal subjective experience — so when it goes back to primal pain one, it'll evoke that initial experience); and the evolved/inductive part (unpacking a chunk with evolved gist evokes a unique fusion of the subjective experience evoked by the meanings of the words in the gist).

CTM becomes consciously aware of more and more broadcasted chunks; the model-of-the-world processor may call attention to this with a very high weight, and the word PT pointing to the broadcasted chunk that calls attention to this becomes a word for "consciously aware" or simply "conscious." As CTM becomes consciously aware of more and more broadcasted chunks, the model of the world will label sketches that CTM is conscious. When the CTM broadcasts "CTM is conscious," it itself, when it unpacks, will feel itself conscious. And this is very much like Michael Graziano's attention-schema theory.
