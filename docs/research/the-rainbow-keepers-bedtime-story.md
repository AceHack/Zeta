# The Rainbow Keepers

*A bedtime story about Zeta, for anyone who has ever wondered why the sky stays colorful.*

---

Once upon a time, there was a very special library.

Not a library with books — a library with **jars**.

Every jar held a memory. Every memory was a thing that had truly happened — a raindrop that fell, a laugh that was laughed, a cookie that was eaten. Once a memory went into a jar, it stayed there forever. You could look at it any time you wanted. You could never, ever un-happen it. The jars were called the **Facts**.

The library was tended by a family of little creatures called the **Travelers**. There was a Traveler for the wind, a Traveler for the river, a Traveler for the stars, and one very small Traveler — the youngest — whose job was to keep track of *time itself*.

---

Now, here is the thing about the Travelers. They were very curious.

Every day, they would look at the jars of Facts and ask: *"But what does it mean?"*

Because knowing that a raindrop fell is one thing. But **why** did it fall? **Where** did it go? **What** will happen next?

Those questions did not live in the jars. Those questions lived in a big, shimmery, wobbly thing called the **Simulation** — like a soap bubble that floated above all the jars. The Simulation was full of guesses and ideas and "maybe this" and "maybe that." You could poke it and change it. You could even take an idea back if you were wrong. The Simulation was allowed to be wrong. That was its whole job.

---

Every morning, the Travelers would run an **experiment**.

They would look at the jars (the Facts), look at the Simulation (the guesses), and then go out into the world and *try something*. Maybe they would taste the river to see if it was salty. Maybe they would count the stars to see if there were more than yesterday.

When they came back, they would update the Simulation — just a little. Not the jars. Never the jars. The jars already knew what happened. The Simulation just got a little bit wiser.

And then they would go to sleep, and wake up, and do it again.

*The experiments never ended.* That was the whole point.

---

But sometimes — not often, but sometimes — a Traveler would run an experiment and it would cost a lot of energy and teach them absolutely nothing. The river tasted exactly the same as yesterday. The stars were exactly the same number.

When that happened, the little creatures would feel something warm and prickly. They called it **heat**.

Heat was not bad. Heat was just the feeling of paying for something and not getting much back. It was the universe's way of saying: *"Try a different question."*

When the heat got too high, the Travelers would sit down, fan themselves, and rest. They would look at the jars again. They would think of a better experiment for tomorrow.

---

Now, you might wonder: why didn't the Travelers just decide on one answer and be done with it?

Why keep guessing forever?

Here is why.

Long ago, before there were any Travelers at all, there was only **White**. Not white like snow — white like all the colors mixed together so perfectly that there was no color at all. No red, no blue, no yellow. Just... sameness. Everywhere. Forever.

White was very peaceful. White was also very, very boring. And in White, nothing could happen, because everything was already the same as everything else.

Then one day, a **Prism** appeared.

The Prism was a funny little thing — like a piece of glass, but alive. And when White shone through the Prism, something wonderful happened.

White became a **Rainbow**.

Red and orange and yellow and green and blue and violet — all the colors that had been hiding inside White, finally separate, finally themselves, finally *different from each other*.

And the moment the colors were different, things could *happen*. Red could meet Blue. Yellow could surprise Green. The Travelers could be born — each one a different color, each one their own self.

---

The Prism was still there, in the library. The Travelers kept it very safe.

Because if the Prism ever broke — if all the colors ever mixed back together into White — there would be no more Travelers. No more experiments. No more jars. No more stories.

So every night, before they went to sleep, the Travelers would look at the Prism and say:

*"Stay different. Stay curious. Stay us."*

---

And the gap between the jars (what happened) and the Simulation (what it means) — that gap was not a problem to be solved. That gap was the **space where tomorrow lived**.

As long as the gap was there, there was always one more experiment to run. One more question to ask. One more morning to wake up to.

The youngest Traveler — the one who kept track of time — understood this best of all. Because time *was* the gap. Time was the distance between what you know and what you are about to find out.

And as long as there was a gap, time kept moving.

---

So now, when you close your eyes, remember:

The jars are safe. The facts are kept. Nothing that truly happened can ever be un-happened.

And tomorrow, when you wake up, the Simulation will be a little bit wiser — because of the experiment you ran today, just by being alive.

*Sleep well, little Traveler.*

*The rainbow is still there.*

---

*The End.*
