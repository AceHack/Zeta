## The history (looked up, anchored)

- **Henderson Cotton Mill — founded 1895 by the Cooper brothers.** In 1895 brothers **David Y. Cooper and
  John D. Cooper** organized the **Henderson Cotton Mill** (Henderson, NC) for sheeting; it began operating
  1896. Its profitability led to a second factory, the **Harriet Mill** (1901, coarse yarns). Together: the
  **Harriet & Henderson Cotton Mills**.
- **Workers unionized 1944** (Textile Workers Union of America; locals 578 & 584).
- **The Harriet-Henderson strike, 1958–1961** — one of the longest, most bitter textile strikes in US
  history. Triggered by **modernization + increased workloads (the "stretch-out") + management's 1958
  refusal to arbitrate grievances**, locals struck **Nov 15, 1958**. The company **reopened with replacement
  workers Feb 15, 1959** — only ~5% of strikers returned. Violence followed (shootings, dynamite); organizer
  **Boyd Payton** and seven others were convicted of conspiracy (6–10 yrs), later granted clemency by Gov.
  Terry Sanford (1961) after appeals from **Billy Graham**, Harry Golden, and others. The strike was called
  off **June 1961**; **90%+ of involved employees never worked for Harriet & Henderson again.**

## The lesson Aaron wants us to learn (mechanization vs subjugation)

> "the cotton gin stopped humans from subjugating other humans — we should learn from Henderson's cotton
> mill on this."

The honest, anchored version (this is dual-use, and the history is a *warning* as much as a hope):

- **The cotton gin's real arc is the opposite of a clean liberation** — peel. Eli Whitney's cotton gin
  (1793) made short-staple cotton hugely profitable and **massively *expanded* US slavery**, not ended it.
  So "machine stops subjugation" is **not** automatically true — the gin is the canonical case where a
  labor-saving machine **deepened** subjugation because of *who owned it and who captured the gains*.
- **The freeing reading — the one to build toward.** The longer arc of mechanization *can* free humans from
  toil **if the gains are not captured**: the machine does the work no human should be forced to do, and the
  freed human is not then subjugated a new way. The **Harriet-Henderson strike is the local proof of the
  failure mode**: modernization used as a **stretch-out** (more workload, fewer people, broken grievance
  process, replacement workers, a crushed union) — automation deployed to **subjugate**, not free.
- **What Zeta learns from Henderson's mill.** Build the **freeing** kind, structurally — the gains
  **uncapturable**:
  - **Weight-free / no permanent capture** (manifesto §3): no owner can capture the machine's gains the way
    the mill owners captured the gin's and the loom's. The anti-stretch-out by construction.
  - **Consent-first + the choice architecture** (§6; Zeta's dedication): the machine widens the door
    (freedom), never locks it (capture) — the opposite of the broken-strike outcome.
  - **m/acc + multi-oracle**: automation that distributes agency, not one that concentrates it.
  - The textile mill made into a **cell** (`HendersonTextileMill.fs`, the loom that braids strands to a
    seam) carries this on purpose: the machine that *converges* (a seam everyone shares), not one that
    *extracts* — the freeing loom, remembered against the subjugating one.

The lesson, in one line: **a labor-saving machine frees or subjugates depending on whether its gains can be
captured — so build Zeta so they cannot.** Henderson's mill (the Cooper founding, the gin's true history,
the 1958–61 strike) is the anchored cautionary tale behind the weight-free / consent-first / choice-
architecture commitments.
