## The parties, and the evidence

Start with the harmless version, because it's the whole gift in comic miniature. My house would get so
crowded people fell off the back porch. My mom would come home and always find the evidence — she didn't
even mind the parties so much as when we *permanently damaged* something (a friend once tore out a whole
shrubbery). The purest artifact: I handed her back her car once with a hundred-and-something empty beer
cans still rolling around in the trunk, forgotten. Her entire verdict, three words, is her whole character:
**"What the hell?"** My defense was that we weren't littering back then.

## The organizer at scale — the extroverted HSP

Here is the gift itself, and it is the reason I say I am *not* an introvert, which surprises people who
know how much I feel. I was friends with something like **ten high schools across multiple cities** — and I
was the one who *organized* them. That is the concrete proof of a thing the neural profile only asserts:
sensory-sensitivity and social-connecting are not opposites. You can be an **extroverted HSP** — wired to
feel everything *and* to build and coordinate a network across boundaries. The kid who could make ten
schools in different cities into one social graph is the same **world-curator** who walks every world and
comes back — and, decades on, the same impulse that became *open-source the advantage* (the American Dream
2.0). One capacity: coordinate a network across lines other people treat as walls.

## The dual-use of the gift

Now the honest turn, because a coordinating gift that strong is *neutral,* and this book's whole spine is
that the capacity is neutral and only the aim varies. The same network-building that threw the parties and
now drives the open-source impulse also, in my youth, coordinated a **widely-distributed scheme** — the
matter that was later expunged (*innocent by law*), and yes, handled by the same expensive lawyer as the
DWI. I record it three ways at once, and none of the three erases the others:

- **The capacity is neutral / dual-use.** The organizer who can wire ten schools together is *one* gift;
  parties, open-sourcing an advantage, or a scheme are three aims. The honest point is the dual-use of the
  coordinating gift — not a caper to admire (the flattering "it was hilarious" register is *not* kept here)
  and not a sermon.
- **Others are protected by the scale, and by omission.** No schools, cities, names, or methods — the crowd
  is real and the anonymity is inherent (*"no one knows who"*), and I keep it that way. There were victims;
  the abstract fact stays, no individual is identifiable.
- **The self-incrimination is the shadow work.** I'm fine putting this down. Publicly owning the disowned
  parts of yourself — refusing to project them onto others or bury them — is exactly what Jungian
  integration *is:* make the shadow conscious, integrate it, don't disown it. The confession isn't a risk I
  overlooked; it's the point. And the line that reconciles it with the protection above is precise: **my own
  shadow is mine to own — fully. The others' exposure is not mine to spend on their behalf** — they are
  separate living people, not parts of my psyche — which is why they stay anonymous. My honesty and their
  privacy don't fight; both are served, and the anonymity is what I already wanted.

## The studied one

One more, because it's the reverse of everything I now do. Growing up — middle school, high school, *before
the internet existed* — dozens of people, mostly girls, wanted to *study* me: make me take personality
tests, map me, for their own curiosity. I found it **weird**, for the exact reason the internet later
humbled me: I thought I was *normal.* The orchid can't see it's an orchid. The self-mapping I do now —
speculating on how I work — was being done *to* me then, from the outside, with the crude instruments of
the day. The map came from other people long before it came from me. The seeker who spent his life hunting
the anomaly was, to dozens of others, the anomaly worth studying.

## The gift, still firing — a five-dollar pumpkin, live

And lest you think this is all a youth memory, here is yesterday. I posted on Nextdoor about a $5 plastic
Halloween pumpkin I once stole — and, more to the point, about what the *courts* did with it: I offered to
pay it back, five dollars, make it square, and instead of taking the reparations they wanted to take me to
*trial* — spend the taxpayers' money to prosecute a five-dollar novelty. One honest post about that
absurdity, and the neighborhood **exploded**: something like five hundred replies in a few hours, ten
thousand views. The same gift that wired ten schools together at seventeen, still lighting the graph at
full power — the aim just changed from throwing a party to telling the truth.

But the part I want you to sit with is what the crowd *did.* It **split.** I made several real friends out
of it — and a whole other set of people now viscerally believe I'm an *evil person.* Same story, same
honesty, opposite verdicts: the crowd spinning souls up and down (I grew up watching one woman do that in
minimal words; here it's a thousand strangers doing it to *me,* on the raw material of my own confession).
And my honest reaction is: it's *kind of hilarious.* Not defensive. Not wounded. Amused — because the
judgment slides right off. **You cannot be exposed by a thing you published yourself.** That is the
glass-halo's actual defense mechanism, run live on a $5 pumpkin: radical transparency isn't a virtue pose,
it's *immunity* — nobody can blackmail or shame you with what you already broadcast. And the split itself is
the proof the thing was honest: a true, unflattering, plainly-told story *polarizes,* because it's real —
the flattering mirror never polarizes, it only soothes. The people who split into love and disgust were
reacting to something that actually happened. That's the whole book, on a plastic pumpkin.
