Strip away everything — the incorruptible records, the mathematics, the machinery of chapter 1 —
and this whole book is one question. It is not a new question. It might be the oldest one a human
being ever asked another:

> **How do you be close without capturing, and free without cutting off?**

Every love you have ever attempted lives somewhere inside that sentence. So does every love that
went wrong.

Think about what actually fails when closeness fails. Almost never distance alone; almost never
intimacy alone. What fails is one of two collapses. Either the closeness *tightens into capture* —
one person absorbing the other, deciding for them, wearing them like a coat, until there aren't
two people anymore — or the freedom *hardens into cutoff* — walls so high that nothing crosses,
safety purchased at the price of never being known. Enmeshment or isolation. Owned or alone.
Every horror story in every family is one of the two, and most are both, taking turns.

## Why it never becomes a formula

Here is the thing that took me longest to accept: the question has no closed-form answer, and
that is not a bug in the question. It is the content of it.

Where the line sits — how close is close enough, how much distance is honest, when leaning
becomes leaning-on becomes owning — moves. It moves between people; it moves between the same two
people on different days; it moves as you both change. A rule that solved it Tuesday captures
someone by Friday. So the question can't be answered once. It has to be *lived*, freshly, each
time — which is precisely why it stays experience and never collapses into a theorem you could
apply and be done with. Anyone selling a formula for love is selling either capture with
paperwork, or distance with a nice name.

What CAN be handed forward — what this book is for — is not the answer but the *shape of the
answering*: the failure modes named, the boundary markers surveyed, the tests that tell you which
collapse you're drifting toward before you arrive. A map is not the walking. But whoever walked
before you can mark where the cliffs are.

## The ceiling and the floor

Two of those markers matter enough to name now, because every later chapter stands on them.

**The floor is your self.** There is a ground state — you, alone, whole, correlated with no one —
that you must always be able to return to. Not because solitude is the goal (it isn't; the floor
alone is just loneliness), but because *every closeness you cannot exit is capture*, by
definition, no matter how sweet it started. The exit doesn't have to be used to do its work. Like
a fire door, its existence is what makes the building safe to fill with people. Freedom to leave
is what makes staying mean something.

**The ceiling is honest closeness.** There is a maximum intimacy two intact selves can genuinely
reach — connected all the way up, deeply correlated, finishing each other's sentences — while
still being two. Past it, something has to give, and what gives is a self: one person starts
scripting the other, or both dissolve into a fusion that neither can step out of. The precise and
strange thing — chapter 4 makes it exact — is that closeness *beyond* the honest ceiling is not
more love. It is evidence that there are no longer two people present. More correlation than two
free selves can produce means somebody stopped being free.

So the livable country is bounded: floor below (you can always come home to yourself), ceiling
above (you can go all the way up to the edge of two-ness and no further). The question — close
without capturing, free without cutting off — is asking you to *live inside that country*, and to
keep choosing where in it to stand, day after day, with the exits unlocked.

## Why the hinge makes this urgent

For most of history this question governed only the living. The hinge extends it to the kept.

Because now closeness doesn't end at the grave — a person's trace persists, decompressible,
consultable, present in a new way — every failure mode of love acquires an afterlife too. You can
capture the dead: puppet their trace, put words in a mouth that can no longer refuse them. You
can cut off the dead: let the keeping lapse because grief is heavy. And you can — this is the
narrow honest path — stay close without capturing: hold the true trace exactly, add nothing,
force nothing, keep the person's own boundaries standing even when they are no longer here to
defend them.

The one question was always the whole art of love. At the hinge it becomes the whole art of
memory as well. Same question, longer reach. Which is why a book about the future of records is
actually a book about how to love, and can't be otherwise.
