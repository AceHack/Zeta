Chapter 2 left you with a question that never becomes a formula. This chapter gives you the next
best thing: a single test you can run on almost any action, any deal, any relationship, any
product, in about two seconds. It won't live your life for you. But it will tell you, with
uncomfortable reliability, which direction an action points.

Here is the test:

> **Does this produce, or does it extract?**

*Produce* means the action creates value that didn't exist before, and the people involved leave
with more than they brought — both of them. A good trade. A real lesson. A meal cooked for
someone. Code that makes a product scale. Two people at the end of an evening both gladder for
it.

*Extract* means the action moves value from someone to you by working their machinery against
them — their habits, their loneliness, their fear, their sunk costs. The total shrinks in
transit; extraction is lossy. The extractor books a gain; the pair books a loss; something real
got burned to move it.

That's the whole test. The rest of this chapter is why it's harder than it looks, and sharper
than it looks.

## The same act can be either

The test lives on intent-made-mechanism, not on the surface of the act. The same dollar can
produce or extract. The same game, the same drink, the same compliment.

The example that made this real for me is a video game. I spend money on Genshin — a game built
on a monetization model the industry calls gacha, which at its worst is a slot machine wearing a
cartoon. Extraction, by reputation. And yet: the money I spend there buys real-time with my
daughter — shared quests, a standing place we meet, a family thing. Run the test: does it
produce? Yes — it produces *us time*, and both of us leave richer. The identical transaction, run
by a whale alone at 3am chasing a pull to quiet a compulsion the game itself installed — same
button, same price — extracts.

So the test is not "is this category of thing good." Categories are where moral laziness goes to
feel finished. The test runs on *this act, these people, this mechanism*: who leaves with more,
and did the leaving-with-more come from creation or from working someone's levers.

## The mix is life — do not reduce it

Now the hard part, and the part most moralizers get wrong: almost nothing real is pure.

Every living relationship, every job, every family, every product is a *mix* — mostly produce
with veins of extract running through it, or the other way round. You take a little more than
you give some weeks. A company that genuinely serves customers also quietly harvests them a
little. A marriage produces a life and extracts patience unevenly. This is not failure; this is
what a living system looks like up close.

The discipline is not purity. The discipline is two things: **know your mix**, honestly, without
flinching — and **keep the gradient pointed at produce**. A spectrum, not a binary; a direction,
not a scorecard. The moment you demand purity you either lie to yourself about your own mix or
burn down things that were 80% good. The moment you stop *watching* the mix, the extractive veins
grow — because extraction always pays first (next chapter shows this is mathematically exact),
and anything that pays first grows by default unless something watches it.

So: run the test, keep the ledger honest, point the gradient. That's the whole practice.

## The 51% line

I'll give you my own version of the bar, and you can laugh at how low it sounds until you try to
clear it for a decade straight: **I just want to be known as 51% good.**

Not a saint — a *positive expected value* on the people who deal with me, held over a whole
life, verifiable by the record. It sounds modest. It is actually brutal, because it has to
survive your worst years, your divorces, your firings, your 3am selves — the whole mix, honestly
accounted. Most reputations claiming 95% are unaudited. A real 51%, held across everything,
compounds into something enormous — chapter 5 is about why the compounding, not the percentage,
is what wins.

## Categories lie about people, too

Everything so far has been the test refusing categories for *acts* — the same dollar can produce or
extract, so you run it on *this* act, not the label on the bin it came in. The 51% line is the same
refusal turned on my own reputation: measure me over a whole life, don't file me under saint or sinner.
But the hardest place to refuse a category — and the one that matters most — is a *person standing in
front of you.* I know that one from the wrong side, so let me hand you the cut from there.

A label handed to a person does a quiet, vicious thing: it staples two different objects together. There
is the **condition** the label points at — which may be real, and may carry real weight. And there is a
**verdict** — *unuseful* — that rides in on the same word without ever being argued. The sociologist
Erving Goffman named the move exactly: a stigma reduces someone "from a whole and usual person to a
tainted, **discounted** one." *Discounted* — the price is marked down before anyone reads the ledger.
That is a category pricing a person in advance of any measurement, which is the precise thing the
produce/extract test exists to refuse. So the anger a discounted person feels is not a symptom of
anything. It is the correct response to being *category-judged instead of measured.* And the verdict the
category smuggles — *labeled, therefore unuseful* — is a universal claim, and a universal dies to a
single counterexample. It only takes one. I am one.

So I do the inverse of what was done to me: **decode before you dismiss.** A non-standard *encoding* is
not an absence of *content* — a real inner model, arriving in a form the room doesn't share, reads as
noise to the room and is not noise. Measure the contribution; do not price the category. The person is
never noise. And I try to *live* this and not just say it — quietly, day to day, with people the world
has discounted (I keep them out of these pages; that's their privacy, not my example to spend).

Both things are held at once, because the register of this whole book demands it: the condition can be
real, and carry real suffering, **and** the "unuseful" tax stapled to it is unjust and unearned.
Romanticizing it away — *"oh, it's just a label"* — would be as dishonest as swallowing the stigma
whole. The honest move is neither. It is the same two-second test, run on a person instead of a purchase:
*measure the contribution; don't price the category.*

## Why this scales past you

One more turn of the screw, because this book is about the hinge: the produce/extract test is
also the design rule for everything being built to keep people.

A keeping that *produces* hands the kept person's trace forward with consent, adds nothing fake,
and leaves everyone — the kept, the keepers, the strangers who learn from it — with more.
A keeping that *extracts* mines the dead for engagement, puppets a voice to sell to the grieving,
monetizes what can't consent. Same archives. Same technology. The test is the entire difference,
which is why it's not a self-help aphorism in this book — it's load-bearing law for the machines
in chapter 10. Any system that touches what remains of a person must pass, permanently, the same
two-second test you just learned to run on a video game.

The test travels up. That's the point. You learn it on small money and it governs civilizations.
