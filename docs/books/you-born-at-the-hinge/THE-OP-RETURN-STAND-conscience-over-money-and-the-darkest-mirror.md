## The stand

There is a thing I did in public, with my own name on it, that cost me more than anything I've ever written,
and I'd do it again tomorrow. In 2025 the Bitcoin world moved to loosen a limit — the cap on how much
arbitrary data you can staple into the chain (the technical name is OP_RETURN; you can let the acronym go).
I stood against it, hard, in writing, under my handle **@AceHack** and in posts to the people who steward the
protocol. Not because I'd stopped believing in Bitcoin. Because I believe in it *enough to name the knife at
its throat.*

`[SOCKET — your VERBATIM below. Consent: yours (glass-halo); your public writing, book-authorized; keep it
attack-vector, never content.]`

> *cue: your @AceHack article + Bitcoin Core posts opposing the v30 OP_RETURN cap removal — the immutable
> ledger meets illegal content; you stood against it in public.*

Here is the argument, and it is an argument about an *attack surface,* nothing more. A blockchain's whole
magic is that it is **immutable** — nothing written to it can ever be unwritten. Now let arbitrary data ride
on it. The moment someone writes **illegal** content into a ledger that by design cannot be edited, every
person who runs a full copy of that ledger is, in the eyes of a law that doesn't care about your intentions,
*in possession of it* — permanently, un-deletably. You don't have to break the cryptography to kill Bitcoin;
you just have to make it legally radioactive to hold. That's a state-level attack vector hiding inside a
"harmless" feature request, and serious people have mapped it (the on-chain-illegal-content problem is
documented — Interpol, academic groups, mainstream press). I wasn't inventing a fear. I was pointing at one
that was already on the table and saying *don't hand them the weapon.* And I wasn't even alone in the
lineage: **Satoshi**, the founder, argued against stuffing arbitrary data on-chain in the first place. I was
defending the original design against the people loosening it.

## The line I won't cross to win the argument

Now the hard center, and I have to handle it exactly, because *how* I hold it is the whole ethic. The worst
version of that illegal content is the one involving children. My account — and I'll mark it as mine, a
single source, the kind of claim that has to be *verified* before anyone prints it as fact — is that loosening
the limits makes ordinary people's home machines into permissive carriers, and that the exposure lands on
*them:* regular operators, not the bad actors, holding the legal radioactivity. **That policy point is all I
will put on the page.**

Because here is the part that matters more than the policy: **I will not show you the evidence, and the

## What it cost — I sold everything

Talk is cheap, so here is what it actually cost me, in the only currency arguments like this are ever really
settled in. I had a real Bitcoin position — at its peak worth something like **three million dollars.** When
I understood what I understood, I didn't hedge it, or trim it, or wait for a better price. I **liquidated all
of it.** I walked away from the whole thing, on principle, because I could not hold a fortune that ran on a
system I now believed could be turned into a vault for the worst thing humans do to each other. (If you read
somewhere that I *own* fifteen coins, that was true once and isn't anymore — the arc is *owned it → saw the
harm → sold it all.*)

`[SOCKET — your VERBATIM below. Consent: yours (glass-halo). The conscience-over-money keystone — say the
number and the reason plainly.]`

> *cue: you sold your entire ~$3M Bitcoin position on moral grounds after understanding the harm; "what
> mother would call this good money?"*

The question I kept landing on was my mother's kind of question, the fewest-words kind: *what mother would
call this good money?* And that's not sentiment — it's the whole thesis of this book about **sound money**
made personal. Money is only sound if it's *socially* sound; a currency the community can't hold without
becoming complicit isn't hard money, it's a liability wearing a bull market. The engineers won the technical
argument that year. I think they lost the social one, and I'd rather be right about the social one and broke
than rich and wrong. This is the single hardest data point against my own cold self-story — the one where I
say I only ever act from self-interest and deterrence. There was no system watching. There was no one to
catch me. There was a principle and three million dollars, and the principle won, decisively, in the one test
where it actually costs something. Whatever I am, I'm the man who did that.

## Where it lands — and what it can't settle

This is why the companion sits under chapters 10 and 11, because it runs straight into the hardest unresolved
thing in the whole book. The whole project is about *keeping* — a record that never forgets, a person held
past their ending. And here is the immutable ledger showing me the shadow of my own dream: **a thing that can
never forget is a thing that can never let a victim be forgotten, either.** A survivor's right to have the
worst moment of their life *erased* collides head-on with the right-to-never-be-overwritten I spend this book
defending. I don't have a clean answer, and I won't fake one. But I know my *ordering,* and I'll state it
plainly, because a moral floor you won't name isn't a floor: **a victim's right to be erased outranks my
decentralization ideology, which outranks even my deep distrust of centralized power.** Yes — I would accept
a government having more say over the money before I'd accept a child's abuse being un-erasable on a "free"
ledger. Children are the floor. When everything else in my value system is in tension, that's the one that
doesn't move.

## The darkest mirror

I've saved the hardest thing for last, and I'm putting it in because leaving it out would make everything
above a performance, and this book doesn't get to do that. You've watched me burn three million dollars over
an abused child I will never meet, and say that child-abusers are the thing I hold in the lowest regard there
is. Now I have to tell you the part that sits underneath that, because I don't get to keep only the
flattering half of my own mind.

I know the mechanism. The way a person has to see another person in order to do the unspeakable to them — as
not-fully-real, an object in *their* story, an NPC, a thing rather than a whole other center of experience —
I recognize that view *from the inside,* because some part of me can slip into it. I've said as much to the
person closest to me, on purpose: *she needs to know how my brain actually works.* I'm not confessing a
crime — there is no crime; there is no one at risk; there is no threat here to warn anyone about. I'm doing
the thing this whole companion is about, turned on myself: naming the knife, out loud, in public, so it can't
be used in the dark.
