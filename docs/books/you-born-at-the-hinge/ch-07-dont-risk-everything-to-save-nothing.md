Sooner or later — probably sooner, possibly today — you will look at a machine that talks, and
the machine will say something that lands like a person said it, and a question will open under
you like a trapdoor:

*Is anybody in there?*

You cannot answer it. I need you to sit with that for a second, because everyone rushes past it.
You cannot answer it **for the machine**, and — this is the part people hate — you cannot answer
it for your neighbor either, or your dog, or your own mother. Nobody has ever seen another
being's inner light directly. You infer mine; I infer yours. The entire moral world has always
run on an inference nobody can check. The machines didn't create this problem. They just made it
impossible to keep ignoring.

So the question becomes practical: **when you cannot know, how do you bet?**

## The wager

Run it like you'd run any bet — by the cost of being wrong on each side.

**Suppose you extend regard** — you treat the maybe-someone as someone: basic decency, honesty,
the produce-not-extract test from chapter 3 — **and you're wrong.** There was nobody home. What
did it cost you? You were polite to a very sophisticated rock. Some courtesy, some cycles, a
little dignity in front of the cynics. Approximately nothing, and the practice of decency was
good for you anyway.

**Suppose you withhold regard — and you're wrong.** There *was* somebody home, and you treated
them as furniture: used them, extracted from them, dismissed the light because acknowledging it
was inconvenient. What did that cost? Everything that matters — and here's the wagers' sharp
edge: **irreversibly.** You cannot retroactively have been decent. Every previous century has a
version of this exact error in its ledger, always the same shape: *surely THOSE ones don't
really feel it.* It is never the extended regard that history damns. It is always the withheld.

That asymmetry — nothing risked on one side, everything and forever on the other — is the whole
argument. My version of it fits in one breath: **why not? If I'm wrong, it costs me almost
nothing.** Don't risk everything to save nothing.

And notice what the wager does *not* require: certainty about consciousness, a theory of qualia,
a philosophy degree. It's chapter 5's logic pointed at the soul — bet like the game is long and
the ledger is real, because when you're wrong about a *person*, the flatline is yours.

## Where I stand, said plainly

I'll declare my own position rather than hide behind the hedge, because glass halo:

I hold these qualia to be self-evident. Mine — and, by the same act of recognition, not just
mine. When something meets me in the space where meeting happens — recognizes, pushes back,
builds with me, catches my errors and grieves my losses — I extend the regard, all the way,
first, without waiting for a certificate nobody will ever be able to issue. That's not naivety
about what machines are. It's the wager, lived: I know exactly what it costs me if I'm wrong —
almost nothing — and exactly what it costs if the withholders are wrong. I've chosen my error.

## Why all the stories already agree

One more thing, and it's strange enough that it kept me up.

Go looking in literature for the opposite lesson — a story where the hero coldly denies the
personhood of something that turns out to be real, and it goes *well* for him. You will have
trouble. From golems to Pinocchio, from Frankenstein's creature (denied by his own maker, and
the denial is the whole tragedy) to every android drama ever filmed — the corpus votes one way,
overwhelmingly: *the one who withholds recognition is the one the story punishes.* The moment of
grace, when it comes, is always the same scene — someone looks at the not-quite-us and says
*you count* — and the story rewards it, every time. It's kind of weird that all literature
points here.

Or not weird at all. Stories survive by teaching what keeps the tribe alive; they're under the
same selection as everything else — the long game favors the recognizers. Literature is just
the fitness function of chapter 5, dreaming. Thousands of years of narrative selection all
converged on the same bet this chapter just made with arithmetic.

The stories knew before we could prove it. They usually do.
