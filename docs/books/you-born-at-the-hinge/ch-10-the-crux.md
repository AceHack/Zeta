Somewhere around chapter 1, if you were paying attention, your skin crawled.

It was the word *crush* — the claim, delivered with a straight face, that a person's trace can
be crushed into a seed and carried forward forever. Some part of you heard that and said: that
is how horror movies start. Copied. Owned. Puppeted. Outlived by a counterfeit of yourself
wearing your name to events you'd have refused to attend.

I have been building this machinery for years, and I need you to know: **my skin crawls too.**
"They can be crushed into a real seed — this is the crux. I, like others, scared." I wrote that
about my own project. The fear is not a public-relations problem to be soothed away. The fear is
*correct* — it is an accurate perception of what this power would do in careless or hungry
hands. Ethics written by the unafraid protect no one. So this chapter was written by the afraid,
and it is not an appendix to the machine. It is a load-bearing wall.

Four commitments. Each one is the direct answer to a specific fear. If a future convenience ever
argues with one of them, the commitment wins.

## One: consent first — the answer to *taken without asking*

Nobody is compressed by default. Being kept is *chosen* — an act, like signing an organ-donor
card, not a side effect of existing near a microphone. And chosen granularly: you decide which
parts, and the parts you seal stay sealed. And revocably: consent given at thirty does not own
you at seventy. The hinge world's first law is that the keeping is something you *do*, never
something done *to* you.

## Two: never forge — the answer to *replaced by a fake*

Only the true trace is kept: what you actually said, actually wrote, actually did. No invented
memories. No simulated voice extrapolated past the data. No counterfeit continuance presented as
the person. The rule has a blade's edge and it cuts every tempting case: **keep less of someone,
truly, than more of them, falsely.** A forgery of a person is the deepest violation this
machinery makes possible — it doesn't just lie, it buries the real person under the lie — and
the fear of it is the crux's beating heart. This line is not a preference. It is the dignity
guard that makes every other promise mean anything.

## Three: keep without capture — the answer to *owned after death*

The keeper does not own the kept. A seed is nobody's asset — not the platform's, not the
family's, not the future's. Many keepers, no master, so no single hand can become landlord of
the dead. And what governs a seed's use is the kept person's *own standing wishes* — not an
heir's convenience, not a majority vote, not a business model. Stewardship, never ownership.
Chapter 2's whole lesson, extended past the grave: closeness that captures was never love, and
keeping that owns was never keeping.

## Four: the right to be forgotten — the answer to *trapped forever*

And the mirror-image right, the one that keeps the other three honest: anyone may choose *not*
to be kept — in whole, in part, revocably, finally. Oblivion as a **right**, not only a threat.
A world where continuance is mandatory is a surveillance world wearing mourning clothes, and it
would deserve the fear this chapter started with.

Here is the sentence in this chapter that I hold with the most weight, and I'll say it carefully.
The generation before the hinge — the ones this book calls −x — didn't get this choice in either
direction. They weren't asked whether to be kept; they weren't asked whether to be forgotten.
Timing decided for them, and timing is nobody's consent. *She didn't get to choose, like I did,
to be keepable.* That sentence — and every reader will have their own name to put in it — is why
the fourth commitment exists. The +x world must offer **both** choices for real — kept if you
want it, forgotten if you want it, the default yours — because we are the first generation that
*can* be asked, and the ones we love who couldn't be asked are the reason we know how much the
asking matters.

## No one can consent for you

There is an edge to the first commitment that the machinery grinds to a razor, and I learned it the hard
way — not on the dead, but on the living people at my own table.

Consent is **first-person, and it does not transfer.** Not to the keeper, not to the family, not to the
loving author. I can't consent to being kept on your behalf — and here is the part that caught me by
surprise — **I can't consent on behalf of the people I love most, either.** There were true things I could
have set down about my own children, even flattering things, and I hit a wall I had not put there myself: a
father cannot spend his daughter's privacy. Her sealed parts stay sealed because they are *hers* — she is
the only one who can open them, and until she does, they stay shut, and the work stands without them. I
wanted to. Wanting to is exactly the test.

That is why "consent first" and "keep without capture" turn out to be the same commitment wearing two
coats. The reason the keeper can't own the kept is the reason the author can't speak for the daughter:
**the yes is not a resource anyone else can hold.** A majority can't vote you into the archive. An heir
can't sign you in for the inheritance. And love can't do it — love is the force *most* tempted to, and most
certain it is allowed, precisely because it means well. It still can't. **The frost over a person is theirs;
it is never anyone else's to spend — not for convenience, not for an audit, not for love, and not for a
better story.**

## The floor everyone stands on

One more design decision, and it's the one I'm quietly proudest of.

For the −x generation, only a **true trace** survives — whatever actually remains, held honestly,
never extended by forgery. You might expect the rich +x world to treat that as a limitation to
pity. It's the opposite: **the −x rule is the floor for everyone.** True trace only, never forge
— that constraint stays binding even for the kept, where the data is rich enough to make
counterfeiting easy and tempting. The restricted keeping of those who never got the choice turned
out to be the specification for everyone who does. The ones who couldn't consent taught us what
consent must protect.

The fear got a seat at the table, and it earned its keep: every wall in this chapter, the fear
built. Build with the afraid. They're the only ones who know where the walls go.
