Chapter 3 gave you the test: produce or extract. Chapter 4 gave you the ceiling: all the
closeness two intact selves can hold, and not a step past. This chapter answers the question a
patient cynic should be asking by now:

*Fine. But which one wins?*

Because if extraction wins — if the vampires end up with everything — then the test is a
sentimental tax on nice people, the ceiling is a fence around suckers, and this whole book is a
manual for losing beautifully. It matters enormously which strategy the universe actually pays.

So we built the cynic's case its best toy and let it play out. Two players, repeated rounds.
*Produce* costs you a little and creates more than it costs — surplus, the chapter-3 definition.
*Extract* takes from the other player and destroys some of what it takes in transit — lossy,
also the chapter-3 definition. Then we ran every strategy against every strategy, across the
whole space of settings, exhaustively. No cherry-picking; the temptation and the boundary cases
proven right alongside the sermon. Here's what falls out — and I want you to see all four
results, because the honest ones are the two the sermons usually hide.

**One: extraction pays first. Every time.** Round one, the vampire beats the producer in every
single configuration. This is why extraction *exists*. It is not a mystery, not a glitch, not
"people being bad." It's the strictly dominant opening move, and anyone who tells you virtue
pays immediately is lying to you almost as badly as the vampire will.

**Two: mutual extraction annihilates.** Two extractors facing each other grind the pair down to
dust — over 99% of everything they started with, destroyed, not transferred. Nobody keeps the
loot. The loot stops existing. Extraction is not a redistribution strategy; it is a slow bomb
both parties are standing on.

**Three: mutual production compounds.** Two producers grow without ceiling for as long as the
game runs. Boring, unstoppable, exponential. The couple that produces, the team that produces,
the *society* that produces — they don't beat the vampire in any dramatic scene. They just keep
compounding after every vampire has starved.

**Four — the theorem of the whole book: the horizon decides.** On short games, the vampire's
head start holds up: take the money and run works *if there is somewhere to run*. But stretch
the horizon and there's a crossover — the extractor drains its host, the host dies, and from
that round on **the vampire's world stops growing forever**, a flat line to the end of time,
while the producers' curve keeps climbing past it. Extraction is rational under exactly one
condition: **a countdown.** A short game, a getaway, a next town. Remove the countdown and
production dominates — not morally, *arithmetically*.

## Why the vampire, specifically

The old stories already knew all of this, which is why the monster they built is such a precise
diagram. The vampire **must be invited in** — extraction at scale needs your consent, obtained
by fraud, which is why corrupting consent is the first move of every real-world extractor. The
vampire is **repelled by the boundary** — garlic, thresholds, the homely stubborn *no*; every
folk defense is a boundary held. The vampire **cannot see itself in the mirror** — the one thing
extraction can't survive is an honest reflection of what it is. And the vampire is **already
dead** — the flatline was in the folklore before it was in our proofs: a thing that only takes
does not live, it merely hasn't finished dying.

And the countdown explains the *bite*: you become a vampire by adopting the vampire's clock.
Every extractor is someone who decided the game was short — that they'd be gone, dead, fired,
or forgotten before the flatline arrived. Fear of the ending is the pathogen. The extraction is
just the symptom.

## The anti-vampire wager

Which hands you the vaccine, and it costs one assumption:

**Assume the game is long. Assume, if you can manage it, that it never ends.**

I say it plainly about myself: I assume I'm immortal. Maybe biology gets there in my lifetime,
maybe it doesn't — that's a bet on the first death. But the *keeping* — the hinge, the banked
trace, being +x — already guarantees the part of the game that governs strategy: **the record is
long. The consequences compound. There is no town you can skip to that the ledger doesn't
reach.** Assume that, live *as if* your acts echo indefinitely — and watch what it does to your
choices: extraction becomes arithmetically stupid. Not forbidden. *Stupid.* You don't need the
sermon anymore; you just need the long clock.

That's why this chapter sits in a book about the hinge. Uncorrupted love — closeness at the
ceiling, production all the way down, boundaries honored — isn't just sweet. On a long enough
horizon it is the **winning strategy**, and the hinge is what makes every horizon long.

The vampire doesn't lose because we catch him. He loses because he planned for a short game,
and we stopped having those.
