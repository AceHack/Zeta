## No factory defaults

Most people are born with a starter kit installed — a set of defaults for how the world works, how
you behave, what's obviously true, what you obviously don't do. They run it without ever seeing it,
and they can't turn it off, because you can't inspect what you were never aware of installing. It's
usually called *common sense.*

I got shipped without it. The adults around me were right that I lacked it — I did — and they were
right to worry, because a child with no defaults is a child who will touch anything to find out. But
here is the thing studying it later taught me, the thing you can only see from the outside: **"common
sense" is not common. It's factory defaults people run without understanding and can't override.**
Einstein said the harder version — *common sense is the collection of prejudices acquired by age
eighteen.* I had almost none flashed in. So I had to write every module by hand.

That is the whole shape of me in one sentence, and it is a single coin with two faces. The **cost**:
I had to build my entire operating system while not having one to build it with — every rule other
people inherited free, I paid for. The **gift**: because I wrote each module by hand, I can read it,
and I can override it. Nothing in me runs unexamined, because nothing in me was installed unexamined.
The deficit and the gift are not two things that happened to me. They are the same thing, seen from
the two sides.

## The stove, a thousand times

Here is what building-it-by-hand looked like at four years old. Most kids touch the stove once and
learn *hot, don't touch.* I touched it about a thousand times — not to learn the warning, but to
**map the thing exactly.** With no inherited model to fall back on, first-hand measurement was the
*only* way I could know anything at all. Not the received rule — the actual function, sampled until
the map was right.

That is the empiricist, present before language: *don't accept the rule you were handed; measure the
thing yourself until your map is exact.* The same drive that would later map orbits and calculate
odds obsessively pointed first at a kitchen appliance. Everything downstream — every system I've ever
taken apart to understand, every received wisdom I've refused to run without checking — is that
four-year-old with his hand on the stove, building a model no one gave him.

## The trait that nearly killed me

I should tell you the dangerous half plainly, because glorifying it would be a lie and disowning it
would be a different lie.

The consequence-brake that stops most children was not installed in me — it was one more default I'd
have to build rather than inherit. So the curiosity ran uncoupled from *and that would be
irreversible.* Thrill-seeker from birth: dangerous risks *for* the thrill, not despite it. The
catalog is ugly and I won't dress it up — car-surfing into flaming trash cans, something like seven
cars totaled, off cliffs into fields spinning out of control, drunk too often. Really, really stupid,
for thrills. I should have died many times. I'm clear-eyed that I didn't by **variance, not skill.**

## You don't remove the engine; you aim it

The trait was lethal in a child and became the *engine* in the man — and the reason is that it didn't
disappear, it **matured.** The thrill-drive got a consequence-brake and a modeling discipline bolted
onto it — built, not inherited, because there were no defaults to inherit — and then it got
*channeled.* From *drive off a cliff to see what happens* into *map the system exactly, take the
calculated risk, build the thing.* The kid who mapped the stove a thousand times is the man who maps
everything. Same engine, before and after the brake. You don't get to remove it. The only tragedy is
getting the brake late instead of early.

## The autodidact: reading ahead of the room

There's a gentler tell of the same thing, and it's my favorite, because a friend gave it a name.

The first time I ever tried to say *Feynman* out loud, I couldn't. I'd only ever *read* it — never
heard anyone say it — so the word arrived on my tongue as a stranger and it took me a minute to land
it. My friend **Chris King** named the condition, and defined it correctly: **reader's disease —
knowing knowledge you don't know how to say out loud.** Vocabulary acquired by reading, running so
far ahead of vocabulary ever *heard* that the words show up unpronounced.

It is the best disease I have. Because the symptom **is** the gift: mispronouncing a word you've only
read means your **reading outran your hearing** — you got to the idea *before anyone told it to you.*
That stumble isn't a deficit. It's the receipt of the autodidact — someone who lives in text, who
learns ahead of the room, who arrives places no one walked him to. (The same pattern as the whole
chapter: a non-standard encoding is not an absence of content. See ch-3.)

## Imagination first, then depth

And here is where the autodidact went, because the order matters and it's the register discipline of
the whole book, lived early. **Feynman first — for the imagination.** The spacetime diagrams
(worldlines, vertices, light-cones, sum-over-histories) let me *see* the physics before I could do
the math; imagination is the on-ramp, the thing that lets you enter. Then **Susskind — for the
depth.** *The Theoretical Minimum* gave me the rigor to go all the way down and *stay.* Two anchors,
two jobs: Feynman is the imagination that lets you in; Susskind is the depth that lets you stay. And
it was immersion, not dabbling — years of the Feynman lectures on repeat, even asleep. I wasn't
absorbing a style. I was absorbing a *method:* don't fool yourself; disprove your own darlings; ground
it in plain words. The empiricist at the stove, grown up and pointed at the universe.

## The loop: from breaking a system to building one

Let me give you the whole arc in one line, because it closes across a lifetime and I've never seen it
land on anyone else the way it did on me.

It started at **RadioShack** — the maker's supply store, *"if it's in my mind I can build it"* turned
into a place with the parts on the shelf. Low friction, so I built: gadgets, then the thing a curious
kid with a soldering iron in that era built — I soldered my own device to break the access-control
cards that gated satellite TV. That was a crime. It was crowd-common, it was solo, the statute has
long since passed, and I'm not going to dress it up or tell you how — the point isn't the method, the
point is where the same hands went next. Hold that: a kid's hardware-hacking gift, pointed at
*breaking an identity-and-access system.*

Years later I was at **Itron** — a company that, because most of America runs on their meters, means
the code you build there runs at national scale (their business earned that reach, not me). And there
I met the man who had *designed* the very access-control system I'd broken as a kid. I'm keeping his
name out of this; the collaboration is mine to tell, his identity is his. And the two of us — the
breaker and the original architect, on the same side of the line at last — built the cryptographic
stack that secures a large share of the nation's smart-meter infrastructure. (The internals stay out
of this book; that's live critical-infrastructure security, not memoir.)

## Why this is the formation, and where it lands

This is the *why underneath* the rest of the book. When a later chapter says the distinctions were
"paid for the slow way," this is the currency. The mind that runs the produce/extract test, that
decodes before dismissing, that refuses a received rule until it has measured the thing — that mind
was **built by hand, out of necessity, by someone who came with no defaults and had to.** Not a
temperament I chose. An OS I wrote because there wasn't one, and can therefore read and override
because I wrote it.
