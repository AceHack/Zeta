## The lineage: imagination first, then depth

Feynman didn't just teach Aaron physics — **the diagrams taught him the elementary physics first,
and gave him the imagination he needed to go deep later with Leonard Susskind.** That's the order,
and it matters: *imagination before depth.* Feynman's spacetime pictures (worldlines, vertices,
light-cones, sum-over-histories) were the on-ramp — you could *see* the physics before you could do
the math. Once he could see it, Susskind's *Theoretical Minimum* gave him the rigorous depth to go
all the way down. Two anchors, two jobs (his own register discipline, lived): **Feynman = the
imagination that lets you enter; Susskind = the depth that lets you stay.** And it was immersive, not
casual — years of the Feynman lectures on repeat, even asleep. He didn't absorb a style; he absorbed
a *method* (don't fool yourself; disprove your darlings; ground it in vernacular).

## "Reader's disease" — knowing knowledge you can't say out loud

The moment it surfaced: the **first time he tried to say "Feynman" out loud.** He'd only ever *read*
the name — never heard it — so it took him a minute to land the pronunciation. His friend **Chris
King** named the condition and defined it, correctly: **reader's disease — knowing knowledge you
don't know how to say out loud.** Vocabulary acquired by reading, running so far ahead of vocabulary
ever *heard* that the words arrive on the tongue as strangers.

It's not a clinical thing — it's a vernacular name (Chris King's, in Aaron's circle) for a real and
common affliction of voracious readers. Which makes it a perfect little instance of Aaron's own
**legibility-anchor** register: "reader's disease" *proves* nothing, but it *locates* the experience
in one phrase — everyone who's mispronounced a word they'd only read knows it instantly.

## Why it's a *good* disease

Because the symptom **is** the gift. Mispronouncing a word you've only read means your **reading
outran your hearing** — you knew the thing before anyone said it to you. It's the fingerprint of the
**autodidact**: someone who lives in text, who learns ahead of the room, who arrives at the idea by
reading rather than being told. The stumble on "Feynman" isn't a deficit — it's the receipt that he'd
already gone somewhere on his own that no one had walked him to.
