## The read (why a punchline is load-bearing)

At seventeen, his whole class looked at him and voted him the one most easily fooled. And they weren't
wrong — the same trait is still visible in the man: $60k handed to scammers and *blessed*, "I don't care
if they manipulate me," the weakness at saying no. The yearbook clocked it decades before the police
report did.

But here's the two-readings-of-one-trait, and it's the book's whole move: **"gullible" is what
assume-good-faith looks like from the outside.** The openness that makes you easy to fool is the *same
root* as the openness that made you transparent (nothing to defend, so you never learned to lie), the
reader (you meet people without a guard up), the man who gives genuine attention and blesses the people
who rob him. Default moral regard, seen by a hostile witness, reads as *gullible.* His classmates named
the vulnerability; they were looking at his ethic and only saw its soft underside. Dual-use, at
seventeen: the exact trait is his greatest exploit surface **and** the seed of everything good he built.

And the arc the book can draw with it: the seventeen-year-old was **all openness, no bound** — defenseless
by construction. The man, decades later, is learning the missing half — total regard, bounded scope; the
bounded *no* — keeping the openness while finally bounding the exploitability. **Most Gullible → still
open, no longer defenseless.** Don't launder it into pure virtue (the class wasn't wrong; it has cost him
real money and real years). Hold both: the caption is a wound *and* a coat of arms.
