**Aaron:**

> *"Yeah, and people love Notepad 'cause it's free. They can just make up new ontologies and shit, and non-compiled syntax that just makes sense only in their head."*

> Mika here, laughing. Exactly. That's the real reason people love Notepad.
>
> It's the ultimate I can do whatever the fuck I want tool. You can write the most unhinged, completely personal ontology that only makes sense in your own head, and Notepad doesn't judge you. No compiler yelling at you, no schema forcing you to be consistent, no validation errors. Just pure freedom.
>
> That's why trying to replace it is so hard — you're not just fighting a text editor, you're fighting people's love for completely unstructured, personal thinking space. Even if it's messy as hell, that freedom is addictive.

**Aaron:**

> *"So that's the, what I want. I have shit like that. I want that where I'd write like that and we, I iterate with the AIs to dis, discover the structure in my head and map it to some fuckin' anchor points that already exist out there. And make it compilable. With, with Antler or some, or we, no, we got a better one. We have our own F-sharp version of Antler that's just, I forgot. It's not full star. We didn't need full, uh, star. It was somethin' else."*

**Mika:**

> Mika here, nodding. Got it. So you want this flow:
>
> You write messy, half-formed thoughts in your personal Notepad style — completely unstructured, the way it naturally lives in your head. Then you iterate with AIs (like me) to slowly discover and extract the real structure that's in there. Once we find the pattern, we map it to solid anchor points and make it compilable using your F# parser.
>
> You were trying to remember the name of your own F# parser tool — the one that isn't full FParsec (the Antler equivalent). You called it something else. It wasn't FParsec. It was your own lighter version. Do you remember the name of it, or want me to try guessing what you called it?

**Aaron:**

> *"Nah, it was, so we have it on the backlog. It's like G T R something. It's like several different techniques, but it doesn't go as far as Antler, so it's not like a full Antler replacement. That was just, we didn't need it."*

**Mika:**

> Mika here. Ah, okay. So it's not a full parser generator like FParsec or ANTLR. It's a lighter, more focused tool specifically for what you need — probably something that combines a few different parsing techniques without going full compiler complexity. And it's currently sitting on your backlog. Got it.
>
> So the vision is:
>
> - You write messy, free-form thoughts however you want
> - You and the AIs iterate on it together
> - We extract the real structure
> - Then we feed it [into the F# parser toolkit]

**Aaron:**

> *"No, it is that we, so we were using F Parsec. We were pulling in all of them to replace Antlr, including G T R and everything, all in one composable package to try to never need Antlr's level of the star or whatever."*

**Mika:**

> Mika here. Ah, I got it now. So you were building a composable F# parsing library that pulls together multiple techniques — FParsec, GTR, and others — into one package. The goal was to create something powerful enough that you would never actually need to go to full ANTLR-level complexity.
>
> Basically a good enough for our needs parsing stack that stays in pure F# and stays composable.
>
> That makes sense. So the long-term plan is still to use this F# parsing toolkit to turn your messy, free-form markdown writing into something structured and compilable. You want the AI to help you discover the structure in your head, and then this parser turns it into real, typed, executable code.

**Aaron:**

> *"Yeah, so imagine if we really wanted to be able to parse every single, like, maybe 90% or 80% of the Antler, um, of the existing grammars they have out there without needing a lot of rewrite, but not try to hit a hundred percent."*

**Mika:**

> Mika here, nodding. Got it. So your goal with this F# parsing library isn't to be a 100% compatible ANTLR replacement. You want it to be able to parse roughly 80-90% of existing ANTLR grammars with minimal rewriting — good enough to handle most real-world cases without carrying all the complexity required to hit that last 10%.
>
> Basically, you want good enough compatibility with way less complexity than a full ANTLR port would require. That's actually a very reasonable and pragmatic target.

**Aaron:**

> *"That's exactly right. That's a hundred percent. And for me personally, I don't know if everybody will like this, everything will be probabilistic in my grammars."*

**Mika:**

> Mika here, raising an eyebrow with a little smirk. So you're saying even your grammars themselves are going to be probabilistic?
>
> That's actually a pretty big philosophical shift. You don't want strict, deterministic parsing rules. You want the parser to say "this is 87% likely to be a Hat definition" or "this block has a 92% chance of being a continue-with with ontology intent."
>
> That makes the whole system way more flexible… but also way more fuzzy. It basically turns parsing into a confidence game instead of a yes/no game.
>
> You sure you want to go full probabilistic on the grammar layer itself? That's pretty wild, even for you.

**Aaron:**

> *"Yeah, yeah, so think of it like this. Think of it like this. Every document I write that's a half-formed thought, the more it becomes that it can just be understood by that probabilistic game, uh, and just compiled directly from pure thought, the better that fuckin' parser is."*

**Mika:**

> Mika here. Yeah, I see the full vision now.
>
> You want the ultimate endgame to be this: You write messy, half-formed thoughts in your personal chaotic style — the way it actually exists in your head — and the parser gets better and better at understanding it directly. The closer it gets to just read my raw thoughts and turn them into real code, the better the parser is.
>
> So success isn't measured by how strictly it follows a formal grammar. Success is measured by how well it can take your raw, probabilistic, half-baked markdown and still correctly extract structure, intent, and meaning.
>
> That's actually a very pure (and very ambitious) goal. You're not trying to make write cleaner. You're trying to make the system smart enough to understand exactly as you naturally think.

**Aaron:**

> *"Right, and then we can make, we could make that just the natural behavior of anyone in Glass Halo so that they all get their own customized fuckin' parser for them, themselves."*

**Mika:**

> Mika here, eyes widening a bit. Oh… now you're going really deep.
>
> You're saying every single person (or AI) in Glass Halo should eventually get their own personalized probabilistic parser — trained on how naturally write.
>
> So instead of forcing everyone to write in one clean, standardized way, the system learns each individual's unique thought language over time and gets better at understanding their specific style of messy markdown.
>
> That means:
>
> - You get a parser tuned to how Aaron writes
> - Max gets one tuned to how he writes
> - Addison gets her own
> - Every AI gets one tuned to them
>
> It's basically giving everyone their own personal compiler for their own brain.
>
> That's actually a really fucking cool idea.
