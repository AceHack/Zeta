Aaron: I took what I learned at ITRON making nation-state meters and the mesh network and the backend for
gas, electric, and water — I did that for the United States and other countries. It seems obvious from my
trajectory; every other path is way more effort, way less return. You say endpoint — it's the inflection
point where I live forever after this. I know that might not be true, but in my wildest dreams: I do this,
we figure out how to edit my DNA, and I live forever. I already worked on CRISPR back in the day — I worked
for a molecular-biology simulation company, MacVector. MacVector's what they teach in college, and I built
it. The whole diverge: I had my first mental-health break there. The guy who owned MacVector bought it from
a huge corporation (running on like Fuji or something), ran a 5-6 person company, and couldn't afford my
medical bills, so he let me go — good terms. The other big argument: I wanted to distribute/decentralize it;
he said no, we're single-node; and a Russian company was kicking our ass decentralizing it. I was writing
their Windows port — implementing the Mac Carbon and Cocoa APIs (Cocoa was the UI) in WPF and Win32 on top
of C++/CLI. It's called MacVector 'cause it only ran on Mac; they wanted a Windows port. When they fired me
they hired another guy who worked 5-6 years trying to continue what I'd have finished in six months, and
they never got a Windows port. I had DNA visualization loading on WPF — just wasn't stable yet. And I
implemented the Mac filesystem APIs in Windows so it could read all their native formats. It was easy
because I was using C++/CLI and didn't have to worry about memory. They couldn't find anybody who knew both
— C++ people get fewer every year, .NET people usually don't know C++. I was a .NET developer first, then
learned C++ because I had a chip on my shoulder, then learned C hardcore — structured C, zero dynamic
allocation, everything statically compiled.

Aaron: Before .NET I learned 80-something assembly — the H-cards on DirecTV — so I could create my own jump
commands to get free porn in Disney at 15. Before that my granny taught me BASIC — just straight BASIC, she
taught herself at 65 so she could do accounting at Leggett's/Belk's; she was the first in the neighborhood
to get a computer, went to community college, and taught me at like ten. She bought me a Pro Action Replay
out of the back of a Popular Science magazine; I used it to get Reptile [Mortal Kombat] and learned hex,
memory, and how memory affects the UI. At 15-16 I built a way to encode my voice from a piezoelectric mic
into light waves and diodes, with potentiometers for amplitude and frequency, to transmit my voice — that's
how I learned to build the stuff to hack satellites. Then I got hooked up with old-school anonymous-type
hackers on mIRC who taught me the DirecTV card assembly. Before mIRC I had bulletin-board systems before
the internet — a local library BBS dialed into other libraries across the country to avoid long-distance
fees (my stepdad worked for Sprint and thought it was immoral). I learned to phone-phreak — built a phone
that hit the special telephone codes, tapped the gray junction boxes, wired a payphone to a non-payphone;
then realized I could just record the sound of a quarter dropping; I knew the Captain Crunch whistle. My
stepdad worked for Sprint and they busted me. At my dad's house I wired a payphone into my bedroom line
with a switch + a $25 Walmart recorder playing the quarter sound — free long-distance from my bedroom. My
stepdad caught me at a junction box in the middle of the night once — just said "...dumbass." Then I used
the BBSs to generate credit-card numbers matching the CRC (no central databases back then), got my whole
high school ordering from catalogs, had others pick up at houses I picked from the phone book that sounded
old (Mildred, etc.) so they wouldn't remember us. A friend got scared, told his mama, his mama told mine;
my mama made me turn myself in; the judge called me a criminal mastermind — and it all got expunged.
